# Round 3: Cross-pollinated remixes (X1–X6)

Each remix combines concepts from different designers so that a new decision appears. The reference directions were introduced only at this stage. Four remixes were prompted by a reference direction, and two (X5, X6) sit entirely outside them. The familiar-game roguelite direction went unused.

---

## X1. Radio Doctor
**Parents:** D2 Radio Sked · B4-b Likeness · B1 The Skerry Forecast
**Prompted by:** free-form character interrogation

**One sentence:** You are the doctor at base camp. At radio calls you book in advance, you question climbers who play down their symptoms so they can stay on the mountain, with only two minutes of battery per call, and you decide who comes down.

*Assumption:* each climber is a character with a hidden physical state, a motive and a memory of how you have treated them. The game can interpret typed or spoken questions and answer in character, consistent with that state. A version without free-form input can use a hand of question cards with the same structure.

**30 seconds of play**
- 18:30. The phone buzzes for the call you booked. Hold to talk. "Mira, how's the head?" "Fine. Bit of a headache, everyone has it up here." Battery: 104 s.
- "Count backwards from twenty for me." She starts briskly. At fourteen there is a gap and an audible breath, then "…thirteen, twelve." Battery: 81 s.
- "What did you have for dinner?" "Wasn't hungry. Had tea." Battery: 70 s.
- "Put Dawa on." Dawa: "She sat down twice between camps today." Mira, in the background: "I'm *fine*."
- Release. On the diagnosis card, set your confidence: exhaustion 45%, altitude sickness 40%, something worse 15%. Choose an order ("Rest day at C2, fluids, call me 06:00") and pin the next call.

**Central decision and how the game responds**
You decide what to spend airtime on and how directly to ask. A direct question gets the answer the climber wants to give. A task (count backwards, walk heel-to-toe across the tent and tell me, describe the view) produces evidence that is hard to shape. Asking a teammate behind the patient's back costs that patient's trust. Hidden states worsen or improve over real hours, and climbers act on your orders and their own temperaments between calls. At the end, your stated confidences are compared with what actually happened.

**Concrete surprise**
At 06:00, Mira describes the sunrise on the ridge as "pink, like the inside of a shell". Those are the exact words she used for the sunset at 18:30. She doesn't know she's repeating herself. Nothing she *said* was a complaint.

**Between rounds / reason to return**
An expedition runs over 5–9 real days, and someone is waiting at the time you booked. Climbers carry their history with you into later expeditions. New characters arrive: a stoic veteran whose "a little tired" means a lot, a client who exaggerates everything, a climber who answers through a teammate's translation.

**Borrowed logic:** Remote expedition medicine. Simple field tests (counting, heel-to-toe walking, appetite and sleep questions), corroboration from teammates, and climbers' known tendency to under-report symptoms when a summit is at stake.

## X2. Shared Kiln
**Parents:** C2 Long Fire · E4 You Cut, I Choose · A2-b Lehr
**Prompted by:** asynchronous inventory battles

**One sentence:** Two potters share one wood firing. One loads all twelve pots, draws a chalk line dividing the kiln and stokes it. The other chooses the front or the back half before anyone sees the results, so the loader has to build a fire she'd be content to lose either half of.

**30 seconds of play (loader)**
- A shared batch of 12 bisqued pots: four tea bowls, a tall vase, three plates, two jars, a bottle, a cup. Ana's secret buyer card: "two pieces with heavy green glaze; one with a drip."
- She stands the vase near the firebox with 1 cm gaps on either side. Flow lines show two jets aimed at the bowls behind it.
- She draws the chalk line between rows 2 and 3. Whatever sits in front, Leo might take, so she moves one of her prized bowls behind the line.
- She stokes (about 2 minutes for 5 days): pine early, a choked firebox at hour 30, recovery, then heavy side-stoking at hour 80 to bring up the back. She bricks the kiln up. The results are sealed.

**30 seconds of play (chooser, later)**
- Leo sees the loading diagram and the firing log (temperature curve, smoke notes, where she side-stoked), but no results. His card: "orange flashing; a fused piece counts double."
- He reads the heavy side-stoking as Ana trying to even out the kiln, and the vase's shadow as a flashing zone. He taps **Back**.

**Central decision and how the game responds**
The loader places pots, draws the line and stokes, aiming for two halves she values equally, because the chooser takes whichever looks better by his own values. The chooser reads the arrangement and the log both for the fire and for the person: where she put the vase says what she was protecting. The kiln follows C2's rules: flame takes the easiest path, ash settles on surfaces facing it and melts to glaze, shadows give flashing, and a tightly packed front starves the back. At unbricking, both hidden cards flip. If each player valued their own half more than the other's, the firing is stamped **no envy**.

**Concrete surprise**
The pots don't respect the chalk line. The jets from Ana's front vase paint three green stripes on bowls behind the line. Her heavy side-stoke, meant to be fair, runs ash glaze off a back shelf and fuses Leo's jar to it, which is worth double on his card and nothing on hers. Her attempt at fairness gave him his best result.

**Between rounds / reason to return**
Roles alternate. The kiln keeps its shelf drips between firings, so pairs start leaving each other useful drips on purpose. Clays and wood change the physics. In stranger play, your sealed, fired kilns become "builds" that other players choose halves of while you choose halves of theirs, and you see the unbrickings later.

**Borrowed logic:** Shared wood firings, where several potters split one kiln and its unpredictable results, combined with the cut-and-choose rule for fair division.

## X3. Drafting Race
**Parents:** C1 Point of Balance · A3 Belfry · D3 Statement
**Prompted by:** conveyor/congestion puzzles

**One sentence:** Sheep pour single file through a narrow race and you swing a heavy three-way gate at its end to sort them by their paint marks, but the gate is slow, each sheep follows the one in front, and too much pressure from the dog jams the race.

**30 seconds of play**
- From above: a holding pen, a funnel, a 12 m race one sheep wide, and a three-way gate into pens L, centre and R. Today's job: blue-marked ewes left, lambs right, everything else centre.
- A gap in the race boards shows each sheep's flank for half a second as it passes. This is your only look at its mark.
- Your right thumb drags the gate, which takes 0.6 s to swing across. Your left thumb sets the dog's pressure at the back of the pen.
- Sheep 1 shows blue, and you're already swinging left as she arrives. Sheep 2, a lamb, is tight behind her. You swing right too late, and it follows its mother into L.
- Sheep 3 balks at the shadow of the moving gate and stops. Three stack up behind it. You ease the dog off, the line settles and starts moving again.

**Central decision and how the game responds**
You decide when to commit the gate for the *next* animal, a sheep ahead, the way a bell is set a stroke ahead. You also decide how much pressure to apply from behind. Rules:
- A sheep follows the one ahead if it's within 1.5 m.
- A gate moving within 2 m of a sheep makes it balk.
- A ewe separated from her lamb turns back against the flow.
- More than three sheep in the funnel causes a jam: one turns sideways and everything stops.

The game answers with flow, through balks, jams, turn-backs and the final count in each pen.

**Concrete surprise**
Holding the dog back on purpose opens gaps in the line. It's slower, but each sheep arrives alone and you have time to swing. Then the old bell-wether goes left, and the next six try to follow her whatever their marks. The fix is to sort her last: hold the gate on centre until she is through, and the line forgets her.

**Between rounds / reason to return**
Yards change: a curved race (sheep flow better when they can't see the end), a weighing crate mid-race, a muddy day where marks are hidden and centre becomes "sort later". New breeds and jobs arrive. One shared daily mob shows how other players ran it.

**Borrowed logic:** Sheep-yard work. Drafting races and three-way drafting gates, sheep following leaders and balking at shadows, and pressure worked from the back of the mob.

## X4. Worn Deck
**Parents:** E2 Kasen · F2 Copy of a Copy · B2 Stemma
**Prompted by:** semantic solitaire

**One sentence:** A patience game of illustrated objects in which every card must share one quality with the card it covers and none with the card beneath that, and every quality you use for a link wears a little off that card before tomorrow's deal.

**30 seconds of play**
- Seven columns. Column 3 shows a Ferris wheel lying on a dryer, linked through "turning".
- Drag a red sock over the wheel and nothing lights. Drag it over column 5's postbox, and "red" glows on the hinge. The teacup under the postbox shares nothing with the sock, so the move is legal.
- Drag a fried egg onto the Ferris wheel. "Round" lights, but the dryer underneath is round too, so the egg slides back with a soft clink.
- A lighthouse links to the wheel through "lit at night" and shares nothing with the dryer. Drop.
- The foundation is a 36-card scroll that follows the same rule. Seat 5 needs a "moon" card (round and gives light), so you start looking for one.

**Central decision and how the game responds**
On each link you choose which quality carries it. Common qualities (red, round) are easy now, but they rule out more for the next card. Rare ones ("waiting", "holds water") score more as harder readings and keep more continuations open. Qualities are not printed on the cards. They light up only when two cards meet.

**Wear:** each link through a quality takes one of three notches off it on that card in your deck. At zero, the quality is gone.

**Concrete surprise**
Your red sock, linked through "red" a dozen times and through "pair" a few, fades to pink and then to just a sock, left with "waiting" and "lost". It becomes the best card in your deck, because almost nothing shares those qualities, so it can follow nearly anything without blocking the next move. Meanwhile your moon card, worn of "round", can no longer sit in a moon seat.

**Between rounds / reason to return**
One deal a day from your own worn deck. New cards arrive with the real season. A card a friend sends carries their wear, and so their way of seeing. Finished scrolls are kept as 36-step chains, such as "Teacup to car wash."

**Borrowed logic:** Linked-verse composition (link to the previous verse, shift away from the one before it, fixed moon and blossom seats), and the wear of well-used things.

## X5. Downstream
**Parents:** A4-b Black Sand · C3 Water Temple
**Prompted by:** none (outside the references)

**One sentence:** Players hold claims one after another down the same creek. Whatever gold you wash over the lip of your pan settles in the claim below yours, so careful panning keeps the gold and hasty panning feeds your downstream neighbour.

**30 seconds of play**
- You hold claim 4 of 9 on a creek. Claim 3, upstream, belongs to a friend. Claim 5, downstream, belongs to a stranger.
- Scoop at the inside of the bend, shake the phone flat, tip it and swirl. The bus is coming, so you swirl fast. "0.06 g washed out." A thin glitter animates downstream into claim 5's gravel.
- The creek map shows glints building in each claim's bars: this week's losses from upstream, now sitting in your gravel.

**Central decision and how the game responds**
You decide how carefully to wash (time against loss) and where to dig (fresh bars, or the slack water where upstream losses settle). You also decide whether to build riffles or a wing dam that slows the water. A dam catches more gold in your claim and starves every claim below. Gold settles wherever the water slows, so each structure redraws the creek for everyone downstream.

**Concrete surprise**
A stranger at claim 2 builds a wing dam, and for a week only water reaches claims 3–9. Then heavy rain at the creek's headwaters breaks the dam (*assume each creek follows the real weather of one chosen place*) and dumps a season of trapped gold into claims 3 and 4. Families find their own use for the rules: a parent at claim 1 pans sloppily on purpose so the gold drifts into their child's claim below.

**Between rounds / reason to return**
Claims change hands each month, so you move upstream or down. Rain reshapes the bars, gems can go into a Pocket Tumbler barrel, and each creek keeps a history of whose gold ended up where.

**Borrowed logic:** Placer mining on a shared creek: claims in sequence, tailings and lost fine gold washing downstream, and wing dams and riffles that change the water for every claim below.

## X6. Almanac
**Parents:** B1 The Skerry Forecast · C3 Water Temple
**Prompted by:** none (outside the references)

**One sentence:** You publish the valley's almanac, a single probability each spring for when the rains will come. The farmers plant according to how far they trust your number, then copy whichever neighbour did best, so your honesty becomes the valley's planting pattern.

**30 seconds of play**
- Spring. The almanac page reads: "Rains before the 4th month: __%". Your evidence: the swallows' return date, the river level, the old priest's notes and last year's pattern.
- You set 60%. Nine terrace farmers each have a threshold: the bold plant early at 55%, the cautious wait for 75%. Blocks 1, 2, 5 and 8 plant early.
- Run the year. The rains come in month 5, and the early seedlings wither while the late planters thrive. The temple wall carves your score.
- Next spring, blocks 1 and 2 copy their late-planting neighbours.

**Central decision and how the game responds**
You decide what number to publish: your honest belief, or a number tuned to produce a spread of planting dates (which shares water) or a synchronised valley (which starves pests). Your reliability chart is public. Each farmer learns your bias and adjusts your number by it. The valley answers with C3's water and pest dynamics, plus a calibration score.

**Concrete surprise**
Years of confident, correct forecasts make everyone trust you and plant on the same day. The pests starve, and the valley has never done better. Then one wrong year at 90% ruins every terrace at once. Your accuracy synchronised them, and your one mistake hit them all together. Meanwhile the farmer at block 7 has worked out that you overstate by about 10% and quietly discounts you, so your numbers have acquired an accent.

**Between rounds / reason to return**
A valley runs for 30 years, under a climate that drifts. New evidence appears (a rain gauge, a neighbour's knee). A successor priest inherits your reputation.

**Borrowed logic:** Seasonal almanacs and probability forecasts, crossed with Balinese subak farmers copying their neighbours' schedules, and the way forecasts change the behaviour they predict.
