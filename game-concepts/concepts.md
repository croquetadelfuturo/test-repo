# Mobile game concepts: invention round

Six designers (separate agents, each with one lens and no view of the others' work) wrote four concepts each. Each designer then expanded another's set, in the pairs A→B, B→C, C→D, D→E, E→F and F→A. I then wrote six cross-pollinated remixes and developed eight concepts for variety. That gives 54 concepts in total: 24 originals, 24 branches and 6 remixes. Raw agent output is in `raw/`, and the remixes in full format are in `remixes.md`.

# Part 1: Eight developed concepts, grouped by experience

## Body and material: what your hands, breath and sleep do

### A2 · Gather
*Interaction designer; expanded by the Wildcard*

**Core.** Molten glass sits on a pipe. Your thumb keeps it turning and you blow into the mic to inflate it. Breath always expands the hottest, thinnest part, so you shape the glass by deciding where to cool it before each breath: drag it across the steel marver to stiffen one face, or hold part of it in the glory hole to soften it. Finished pieces go into the annealer overnight, and uneven walls crack.

**First session.** Order 1 is "a round float, any size." Turn the phone sideways and tip it forward, and the pipe comes out of the furnace with a glowing gather. You've been rolling since the first screen. When you stop to look, the glass sags toward the floor of your actual room, so you roll again. Lift the phone and puff. The bubble opens lopsided, fat on the side you held toward the furnace mouth, and that's the first rule you learn by seeing it. Order 2 is a cup. You drag the bottom across the marver until it sizzles and dulls to orange, and when you blow, the base holds while the walls swell. Crack it off, put it in the annealer and close the app. Next morning the app opens on the annealer door. The float survived. The cup greets you with a *ping* and a hairline crack across its 8 mm base.

**From rules to surprise.**
- **First sessions:** roll, cool, breathe, reheat.
- **Then thickness:** walls become a delayed consequence. You start blowing two short breaths with a reheat between them to even the walls out.
- **Then colour trails:** temperature gets a second job. A thread sinks in above about 900 °C and sits on the surface as beads below that, so where you cool shapes the pattern as well as the form.
- **The surprise:** the breath rule meant for bowls turns out to be a sculpting language. Reheat a 1 cm spot for a second and a half, puff, and a bulb rises from the side. Four more and you have a pig. Thin ears on a thick body make an overnight bet.

**The thing you'd show someone.** One puff, then your thumb over the mouthpiece. The trapped air keeps heating, and the bubble keeps swelling on its own while you roll. Your breath is still working after you've stopped.

**Two directions.**
- *The hot shop.* Glass passes between players' roles. One player blows. A friend's phone is the assistant's bench, with punty, bits and jacks. Another player runs the overnight annealer (A2-b Lehr), and a third cold-works and engraves.
- *Other breath crafts.* The same "breath goes to the hottest place" rule applies to lampworked beads on a mandrel, or to neon tubes bent over a ribbon burner and blown to keep the bore open.

**What a delighted player might pay for.** A box of colour rods with their own behaviour, such as a gold ruby that comes out amber and strikes red only on a second reheat.

### F4 · Nilometer
*Wildcard; expanded by the Social designer*

**Core.** Before bed you plant the terraces of a small river kingdom. The phone lies face-down on the mattress and records your movement. The first third of your sleep is the Inundation, the middle third the Growing season and the last third the Harvest, and the size of your movements sets the height of the flood. In the morning the nilometer shows how the year went.

**First session.** It's 23:05. You see a valley in cross-section: the river, five terraces and a stone staircase marked in cubits. The game doesn't know how you sleep, and it turns out you don't either. You hedge: barley on terrace 2, flax on 3, lentils high on 5. The phone goes down beside the pillow.

At 07:12 the staircase fills to 14 cubits, "Sufficiency", and your night pours across the valley as a wave:
- A burst of turning at 23:20 floods terraces 1 and 2 during the Inundation, which silts the barley.
- Terrace 3 stays dry, so the flax comes up thin.
- A restless hour at 05:40 reaches terrace 3 in the Harvest and flattens what flax there was.
- The lentils never see water.

The scribe tallies barley 120 sacks, flax 15, lentils 30, tax 70. The chronicle writes its first line: *In the first year of the reign, the river slept through its middle months.*

**From rules to surprise.**
- **First week:** you learn the mapping.
- **Second week:** you learn yourself. Fridays are high floods, late coffee drowns the Growing season, and the night before an early flight is a deluge. You start planting for the sleeper you expect, and sometimes sleeping for the crop you planted.
- **Then tools:** a basin dike turns a six-minute stir into a full irrigation. Canals drawn at a high intake fill only on restless nights and send your worst hours to a dry orchard.
- **The surprise is the household:** the chronicle has been calling a spike at 04:10 every night "a hippopotamus broke the canal". It's the cat landing on the bed, and players build a dike where the hippo strikes. A partner's 2 a.m. roll floods terrace 2, and couples negotiate who takes the sofa in harvest week.
- **Later:** after seven high floods in a row the river shifts channel, and the valley you'd learned is gone.

**The thing you'd show someone.** The morning replay: last night drawn as a flood moving across the fields, with an omen in the chronicle that turns out to be your cat.

**Two directions.**
- *Two Rivers* (F4-b). Two people sleeping in different cities are the two rivers of one plain. Whoever wakes first decides whose night waters whose fields, and every transfer carries salt.
- *Other rivers, same night.* In a glacier valley, stillness builds snowpack and restlessness melts it in spring. In a tidal delta, the rhythm of your movement matters, not its height.

**What a delighted player might pay for.** A new river valley with its own rules for reading a night.

## Working out what's true

### B3 · Segregable
*Information designer; expanded by the Interaction designer*

**Core.** You are a declassification reviewer. You swipe black bars across old documents so a requester, who has their own dossier, can't piece together a protected secret, while still releasing more than 70% of the text as the rules require. Every bar needs an exemption code that fits. Three times per document you can **Run Requester** and watch their pen string phrases toward the secret.

**First session.** Document 1 is a 1961 harbourmaster's memo with a red tab: *PROTECT: identity of the informant.* The dossier holds the crew list of the *Kestrel* and a newspaper clipping about a seized cargo.
1. You swipe across "Henrik Lund" and code it E6 Privacy. Release: 94%.
2. You run the requester. The pen joins "the ship's cook" on line 4 to the crew list, which has one cook, H. Lund, and pins his photo. The name was never the secret. The role was.
3. You bar "the ship's cook" too and run again. The pen tries "joined at Bergen" with the clipping, and the string falls slack. Release: 88%. Filed.

Document 2 is typewritten. You bar a four-letter name, and the pen measures the bar, writes "4 letters" and checks the dossier for four-letter names.

**From rules to surprise.**
- **Documents 1–3:** a name is rarely the secret.
- **The next few** teach what leaks:
  - the width of a bar in a monospaced font;
  - a code that has to fit (the appeals clerk restores a bar you coded E1 Security on a tide table);
  - a ghost overlay of another agency's earlier release, whose gaps you have to cover.
- **Then tools:** a fat marker that overshoots, a fine one that streaks, and brackets that replace a span with a description. "[a woman]" quietly removes Teodor from the list of suspects.
- **The surprise:** a stuck player releases *more*. Publishing the full passenger list of the last Thursday boat turns "arrived on the last boat" from a fingerprint into a crowd of nine, and the chain goes cold.
- **Later in the campaign,** your own past releases turn up in the requester's dossier.

**The thing you'd show someone.** Running the requester: the pen pulls string across the corkboard, phrase to phrase, toward the empty photo slot, and stops one link short.

**Two directions.**
- *An archive across decades.* One fictional port town over thirty years, with secrets that interlock, so what you released in 1961 decides what can still be protected in 1974.
- *Reviewer against requester.* Your finished redactions become other players' puzzles for 24 hours, and theirs become yours. The other side of the desk (B3-b Passed by Censor) becomes its own mode.

**What a delighted player might pay for.** A case file: forty documents from one fictional institution, with its own exemption rules and one secret running through all of them.

### X1 · Radio Doctor
*Remix of D2 Radio Sked + B4-b Likeness + B1 The Skerry Forecast; prompted by free-form character interrogation*

**Core.** You are the doctor at base camp. You book radio calls in advance and get about two minutes of battery for each. On those calls you question climbers who play down their symptoms so they can stay on the mountain. Direct questions get the answers they want to give. Tasks such as "count backwards from twenty" produce evidence they can't shape. You commit to a confidence in each diagnosis and to an order, and the climbers act on it, and on their own temperaments, until the next call. *(This assumes characters with a hidden state who answer typed or spoken questions consistently. A question-card version has the same structure.)*

**First session.** It's expedition day 1, a real Monday.
- **12:00.** Three voices check in from Camp 1, and nobody is sick. You don't know it yet, but this is the most useful call of the week. You ask each of them to count backwards from twenty, and Mira does it in six seconds, laughing.
- **18:30.** Mira mentions a headache, "normal altitude stuff". You ask what she ate ("soup"), put mild altitude sickness at 70%, order a rest day and book 06:00.
- **06:00.** Her count takes eleven seconds, with a gap and a breath at fourteen. She says she slept fine. You spend twenty seconds asking Dawa to step outside the tent. He tells you she was sick at three and asked him not to tell you. You order her down to Camp 1 with Dawa.
- **Next call.** She's short with you, and it's harder to get anything out of her.

**From rules to surprise.**
- **First the rules:** calls, battery, questions, tests, confidence, orders.
- **Then the people:** Dawa never complains, so his "a little tired" means a lot. Priya exaggerates. Mira hides. You learn that a baseline matters more than any single answer.
- **Then the radio** turns out to carry more than words: a gap for breath, a cough behind "all fine here", a sentence repeated.
- **The surprise:** your questions change your patients. Going around Mira to Dawa makes her guarded. "You're feeling OK, yes?" gets a yes and makes her a little more sure she is. Climbers you treated straight tell you more on the next expedition.

**The thing you'd show someone.** At 06:00 Mira describes the sunrise as "pink, like the inside of a shell". Those are the exact words she used for the sunset twelve hours earlier, and she hasn't noticed. Nothing she said was a complaint.

**Two directions.**
- *Other places where help is a voice away.* A ship's medic on a satellite phone, an Antarctic winter station, a lighthouse keeper. Each has its own conditions, its own tests and its own ways people hide.
- *Played by two people.* One draws a hidden condition and a motive and plays the climber. The other plays the doctor with the same battery.

**What a delighted player might pay for.** A new expedition: a winter ascent where cold injuries replace altitude illness, the battery drains faster, and there's a new team to get to know.

## With people you know

### E3 · Long Hum
*Social designer; expanded by the Time designer*

**Core.** A family or a group of friends keep one shared hum going for as long as they can, whether they're together or in different cities. Each person hums into their own phone with an earbud in. Nobody can hold a note forever.
- A fade leaves a clean end.
- A sudden stop frays it.
- A loud entry ties a knot.
- If every voice stops at once, the thread snaps.

At set moments the thread must pass through a *needle*, which means every voice at once for three seconds.

**First session.** Sunday, 21:00. Theo starts a thread for Gran in Cork, Ruth in London and Ash in Melbourne, with one rule: no talking once it begins.
1. **First thread, 23 seconds.** Everyone comes in at full lungs and everyone runs out together. Snap.
2. **Second thread, 58 seconds.** Gran starts alone and the others join one by one. At 0:31 Theo stops dead, which frays the thread and costs three seconds, but Ruth is still going.
3. **Fifth thread, 1:47.** By now someone has noticed that fading slowly costs nothing and that the others can hear you leave, so they hold a beat longer when someone fades.
4. **The first needle.** A ten-second warning comes at 1:20. Everyone fills their lungs, hums through it, and everyone is empty at 1:34. Snap, and laughter when the call opens.

The cloth shows five short rows.

**From rules to surprise.**
- **The rules are three:** keep sound going, leave gently, come back softly.
- **The real skill,** staggered breathing, is never explained. The snaps teach it.
- **Roles appear.** Gran anchors. Ash becomes the one who breathes early before a needle and carries the thread alone while everyone else gasps.
- **Chord rounds** give each voice a note, and the thread is gold only while all four sound.
- **Bends** let one voice lead a slow rise while the others follow, braiding the strands.
- **The surprise comes a few weeks in.** With four people staggering their breaths, nothing ends the thread except deciding to. Families leave it running while they wash up and knit. Needles become the moments everyone gathers, and the snap becomes how they say goodnight.

**The thing you'd show someone.** A needle: four voices swell together for three seconds, and one of them has already slipped out and back in, so the thread survives everyone's gasp.

**Two directions.**
- *Nine O'Clock Hum* (E3-b). One hum travels west around the world with 21:00. Each player hears three seconds of a stranger and adds one breath, starting on the note it ended on.
- *Parts.* Hummed tunes are split across voices so each person owns certain notes, as in a handbell choir, and the group can only play the song together.

**What a delighted player might pay for.** The group's cloth as a real object: a scarf or tea towel woven from a season of rows.

### X2 · Shared Kiln
*Remix of C2 Long Fire + E4 You Cut, I Choose + A2-b Lehr; prompted by asynchronous inventory battles*

**Core.** Two potters share one wood firing. One loads all twelve pots, draws a chalk line across the kiln and stokes it. The other sees the arrangement and the firing log but not the results, and chooses the front or the back half. The kiln follows these rules:
- Flame takes the easiest path.
- Ash settles on whatever faces the flame and melts to green glaze.
- Pots in shadow flash orange.
- A tightly packed front starves the back.

Each player has a hidden buyer card. The results arrive when you unbrick the kiln in a later session.

**First session.** Ana and Leo used to share a wheel at an evening class.
1. **Ana loads.** Her card reads "two pieces with heavy green glaze; one with a drip." Tea bowls belong in the front row, facing the fire.
2. **She draws the chalk line,** and the game stops being about her pots. Whatever she puts in front of it, Leo might take. She moves one bowl behind the line. She stands the tall vase in front with 1 cm gaps beside it, so flame jets onto the bowls behind.
3. **She stokes:** pine early, a choked firebox at hour 30, a recovery, then heavy side-stoking at hour 80 to bring up the back. She bricks it up.
4. **At lunch Leo opens the diagram and the log.** His card says "orange flashing; a fused piece counts double." The heavy side-stoke looks like Ana evening things out. He picks **Back**.
5. **Next evening they unbrick together,** brick by brick on both screens:
   - In front, bowls dripping green (Ana's).
   - Behind the line, two bowls with three green stripes from the jets and plates flashed orange in the vase's shadow.
   - A jar fused to the shelf by a run of ash glaze, which is Leo's and counts double.

Both cards flip. Each of them valued their own half more, so the firing is stamped *no envy*.

**From rules to surprise.**
- **The first firings** teach flame and ash.
- **Then the other person:** a loading is a message, and where someone puts the tall vase tells you what they're protecting.
- **The surprise:** pots don't respect the chalk line. Front pots shape back pots through jets, shadows and a starved back row, so the loader can give gifts or do damage across the line. A well-meant side-stoke can hand the chooser their best result.
- **The kiln remembers, too.** A drip left on shelf B4 marks whatever stands there next time, and pairs start leaving each other drips on purpose.

**The thing you'd show someone.** The joint unbricking: two phones, one kiln, the chalk line still visible, and the pots appearing one brick at a time.

**Two directions.**
- *Stranger kilns.* Your sealed, fired kilns become builds that strangers choose halves of, while you choose halves of theirs. You see each unbricking later.
- *The communal firing.* Four potters share one big climbing kiln, divided by "last diminisher": each in turn may trim the section on offer, and the last one to trim must take it.

**What a delighted player might pay for.** A named kiln with its own fire, such as a salt kiln that glazes whatever the vapour touches or a two-chamber climbing kiln. The pair keeps it, shelf drips and all.

## Keeping something going for other people

### F3 · Night Orders
*Wildcard; expanded by the Social designer*

**Core.** A sailing ship takes about 250 four-hour watches to cross an ocean. You command its deck for one watch:
1. You give twelve orders.
2. Forty seconds of bells and weather play out.
3. You write an 80-character log entry and set one concern flag for the stranger who takes over.

You may never learn how the voyage ends.

**First session.** Your first ship is the *Margaret Ann*, 31 days out of Falmouth for Valparaíso, and you have watch 87. Eight bells. The last three log entries read:
- "Glass falling. Topsail patched. Watch the bosun. (K.)"
- "Squall at 2 bells, shortened sail. Bosun quiet. (M.)"
- "Calm all watch. Water 60%. (R.)"

The barometer reads 1004 and is falling. You spend your orders:
- 1 to reef the topsail.
- 1 to turn a point south.
- 2 to sound the water casks. Cask 7 is leaking, and water is really at 54%.
- 1 to halve the ration, which makes crew mood dip.
- 6 of the remaining 7 to restow the hold so the casks sit low and aft. That pays off ten watches from now, in watches you won't stand.

You stand the watch. A squall comes at six bells, and your reef holds. Your log reads "Cask 7 leaking. Water rationed. Restowed aft. Squall held reefed. (J.)" Your flag is water. In the margin you draw one pencil stroke, an arrow at the hold. Tomorrow you'll be on another ship.

**From rules to surprise.**
- **First the rules:** orders, watch, log, flag.
- **Then reading:** which officers are reliable, what K.'s flags tend to mean, which standing orders still make sense.
- **Then** spending orders on futures you won't see.
- **The surprise is that logs make crises as well as report them.** "Watch the bosun" sends six officers in a row to search his locker. Every search costs his loyalty, and by watch 70 there's a go-slow that the warning itself created.
- **Some days later,** a notification: the *Margaret Ann* has made Valparaíso. The full log arrives with a chart of 250 coloured watches, yours highlighted, and watch 103's entry: "Water holding thanks to 87's ration."

**The thing you'd show someone.** The landfall chart: a whole ocean crossing drawn by strangers, with your four hours in it and someone else's note quoting you.

**Two directions.**
- *More roles on the same ship.* A navigator extends a pencil position no one can check (F3-b Dead Reckoning), alongside a surgeon and a cook. Each keeps their own log for their own successor, so one ship carries several relays at once.
- *Other long efforts.* The same relay could run on a cathedral built over generations, where you are master mason for one season, or on a lighthouse service or a generation ship.

**What a delighted player might pay for.** Commissioning a ship: naming it, choosing its route and cargo, then receiving its complete log at landfall, written watch by watch by strangers.

### F1-b · Jumpers for Goalposts
*Branch of F1 Whistle, by the Social designer*

**Core.** You're the oldest kid at a park kickabout with no rulebook. You settle arguments and approve or refuse the rules other kids invent mid-game. Every ruling goes into your notebook and becomes precedent. Each kid has a fairness meter. When it empties, they take their jumper home, and that jumper was a goalpost. The evening's score is minutes played times kids still there at dark.

**First session.** 6:40 p.m., with ten kids and four jumpers. Your notebook is blank.
- **0:40.** The ball goes over the left jumper at about head height. "Under the bar!" "There IS no bar!" You rule that the bar is the tallest player's head (Dev's, 1.6 m), so it's no goal. That's line one, in your handwriting.
- **Rush goalie.** Mia, 8, holds up a card proposing it. You allow it, both keepers abandon their posts, it's 4–4 in three minutes, and every meter rises.
- **Three corners.** Dev, losing, proposes "three corners make a penalty." You allow that too. Within ninety seconds the Blues stop shooting and start blasting the ball off Red shins. Every Red meter drops. Sam's empties and he takes his jumper home, so the left goal is now one jumper and a water bottle.
- **The patch.** Jas proposes "off your own shin doesn't count", and you accept it.
- **Tea time.** Someone's mum shouts "Tea in five!" *Next goal wins.* Final score: 34 minutes × 8 kids.

**From rules to surprise.**
- **First:** rulings and meters.
- **Then precedent binds you:** "You said 1.6 was over!"
- **Proposals are evidence:** a rule tells you who's losing and what they're good at.
- **The surprise is that rules combine.** Rush goalie plus three corners makes an exploit nobody intended. Patches pile up, and the notebook turns into a constitution with loopholes kids can quote at you.
- **Then other streets.** Another street's kids arrive with their own notebook, and the two have to be merged before kickoff.
- **Over seasons kids get older.** Mia proposes sharper rules at eleven. Dev moves away and comes back one summer quoting a rule you repealed.

**The thing you'd show someone.** Two kids sprinting at you mid-game with opposite claims, and the notebook flipping open to your own handwriting from last Tuesday.

**Two directions.**
- *Other games with unwritten rules.* Back-garden cricket (over the fence is six and out; one hand one bounce), tag (no tag-backs) and marbles (keepsies or friendlies). Each has its own folk law and its own kids.
- *Growing out of it.* You age out of being the oldest kid. A kid you once ruled for becomes the arbiter, quoting your rulings, while you watch from the bench.

**What a delighted player might pay for.** A new street: new kids, and an old notebook someone's older sibling wrote years ago, full of rules you have to honour or overturn.

# Part 2: Concept bank

Each original appears with its round-2 additions (marked R2) and the branch another designer grew from it. The eight above are listed by ID only. "Strange" marks the concept each designer flagged as the one a conventional brainstorm might drop.

**Independent convergences.** Designers who never saw each other's work landed on the same ideas several times:
- **Planted memory.** B4 and D3 both use a four-second glimpse followed by questions that plant false details, and both include a vehicle "hitting the bin". B4-b and D3-b both turn the player into the interviewer.
- **Change ringing.** A3, A3-b and D4-b.
- **Textual criticism.** B2 and F2-b.
- **Logbook relays between strangers.** F3, C2-b and F3-b.
- **Friendship-bread starter.** D1-b matched a remix I'd planned, so I dropped my version.

### A · Interaction designer (expanded by F)
- **A1 Strung.** Six fingertips are the pegs of a string figure: slide to pass under a strand, hop to pass over, and the figure and its chord appear only when you spread your hands. *R2: rock a finger to bend strands into a playable harp. One silhouette can be reached by move sequences that sound different, so people learn to solve by ear.*
  - **A1-b Fingerloop.** Braid an endless cord with eight loops on your fingertips, taking each one straight or reversed. The pattern shows up five passes late, and 12-pass letter braids can carry hidden messages.
- **A2 Gather.** Developed above.
  - **A2-b Lehr.** You never blow glass. You load other players' pieces into the overnight annealing kiln and draw one cooling curve that has to serve every thickness. Slumped pieces come back as requests.
- **A3 Belfry.** Ring one bell in a band of six. Each pull sets when the bell sounds on the next stroke, so you change places by planning a swing ahead. *R2: call changes, with one thumb ringing and the other calling. Ghost bells keep ringing in friends' towers long after their ringers stop playing.*
  - **A3-b First Peal.** Invent a ringing method on a place-notation grid. True methods go into the shared library under your name, and strangers ring them.
- **A4 Pocket Tumbler** *(strange)*. Your phone is a rock tumbler barrel. Your walking, commuting and fidgeting grind stones through four grit stages over a week, and you judge progress by the sound when you shake it. *R2: carry it upright to roll stones round, or flat to rattle faces flat. Households split the stages by lifestyle: the runner does coarse grit, the bus commuter does polish.*
  - **A4-b Black Sand.** Pan a creek by shaking, tipping and swirling the phone. Gold settles where water slows, and real local rain restocks the creek.

### B · Information designer (expanded by A)
- **B1 The Skerry Forecast.** From sparse station reports, post percentage forecasts for a small archipelago. At dusk, a Brier score and villagers who act only on bold calls judge your honesty. *R2: paint forecasts onto an hour strip with finger pressure. An honest gale call keeps in harbour the boats whose reports you need.*
  - **B1-b Glass.** The phone's pressure sensor becomes a household barometer. Tap the glass, look at the real sky, give one honest rain percentage, and let the real day score it. You also learn that a lift ride is not a storm.
- **B2 Stemma.** Rebuild the family tree of 5–7 corrupted manuscript copies from their shared errors, and through it the lost original. *R2: write conjectures that no copy contains, or put daggers around what defeats you. Forgers learn to plant "harder readings".*
  - **B2-b Palimpsest.** Tilt the phone to rake light across scraped parchment and trace the erased text within a conservator's light budget.
- **B3 Segregable.** Developed above.
  - **B3-b Passed by Censor.** Write two-sided letters home from a wartime port in a family code. The censor's razor removes both sides of every cut, so you place your clues, and your bait, to suit.
- **B4 Assumes Facts** *(strange)*. See a scene for four seconds, then testify while a lawyer slips false details into the questions. Object at the right moments, then survive a cross-examination built from your own answers. *R2: sketch exhibits in pencil (safe) or ink (sworn). Players who learn how often each detail appears start answering from statistics, and "schema" lawyers exploit that.*
  - **B4-b Likeness.** As a police composite artist, question a witness and build a face. Leading questions, and every look at your draft, pull her memory toward your drawing, and the line-up shows it.

### C · Systems designer (expanded by B)
- **C1 Point of Balance.** Herd five sheep through a trial course with four whistle commands to a dog whose position pushes on the flock. A hidden lead ewe changes how the flock responds. *R2: compose your own whistles (short ones are fast, long ones are clear). A windy week teaches dog and handler to whistle early, which ruins the shed on calm days.*
  - **C1-b Look Back.** Two players. The handler sees the whole field and can only whistle. The dog sees each sheep's fear from the grass and decides what each whistle means.
- **C2 Long Fire.** Pack pots into a tunnel kiln so flame and ash follow the paths your arrangement creates, stoke in rhythm, and unbrick next session. *R2: wrap pots in straw or set them on shells for fire marks. Players sacrifice cheap pots to leave useful shelf drips for later commissions.*
  - **C2-b Night Shift.** Six players share one five-day firing in short stoking shifts. They see the kiln only through spy holes and pass on what they know in a 20-tile logbook.
- **C3 Water Temple.** Each year you set the planting calendars of two terrace blocks. The others copy their most successful neighbour, and water and pests travel through the valley as a result. *R2: tie "marriage threads" between distant blocks, or sacrifice one block as a firebreak against a superstitious calendar.*
  - **C3-b Kliwon.** Write decrees as sentences in interlocking 3-, 5- and 7-day weeks. Farmers copy the words, not the dates, so changing one word can push some blocks five weeks late.
- **C4 Caller** *(strange)*. Call contra-dance figures in time with the band without ever moving a dancer. The progression your sequence produces is the puzzle. *R2: choose full, short or silent calls against the dancers' hidden memory. A sequence that freezes the progression becomes a matchmaking tool.*
  - **C4-b The Collector.** Watch a village perform a dance you don't know and write it down as calls, telling the dance apart from lost dancers and local variants.

### D · Time designer (expanded by C)
- **D1 Proof.** Run a one-oven sourdough bakery in real time, using feeding ratios, warmth and the fridge so doughs ripen when you'll actually be looking. *R2: score the dough with a finger, and a well-timed loaf opens along your lines. An owner with an unpredictable day drifts into running "the focaccia place".*
  - **D1-b Friendship Bag.** Tend a bag of starter from another player on a 10-day schedule, add one pinch, split it four ways, and watch its family tree grow through strangers' kitchens.
- **D2 Radio Sked.** Lead an expedition over 5–9 real days, talking to climbers only at radio calls you book. Between calls they act on your standing orders and their own temperaments. *R2: build standing orders from chips while the cold drains the battery. Crowds turn the best weather window into the worst.*
  - **D2-b Grey Line.** From a remote radio station, relay written messages around the world through paths that open as day and night move, using skeds booked with other players.
- **D3 Statement.** You get one four-second look at a street corner each morning. An investigator questions you through the day, and the longer you wait, the more a correct answer is worth. *R2: a free sketch "swears" details so later questions can't plant them. # Three generative questions

1. Which jobs exist mainly so that other people's activity goes well (callers, referees, censors, dispatchers, stage managers, lifeguards), and what would it feel like to play one for people who never notice you're there?
2. What else could pass from stranger to stranger, the way a starter, a tune, a ship's log or a hum does, and become worth more because nobody controls it? And what is the smallest act of care a player could add before passing it on?
3. What do people trust without checking (a map, a recipe, a family story, a scoreboard, their own memory of this morning), and what game lives in the gap between that copy and the original?
