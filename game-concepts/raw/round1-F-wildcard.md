# F. Wildcard: Round 1 Concepts

---

## F1. Whistle

**1. One sentence:** Two computer-controlled football teams play a full match and you are only the referee. Your score is how good the match was, not who won it.

**2. Assumption challenged:** The player is a competitor who wants one side to win.

**3. 30 seconds of play:**
- **0:00** You see a top-down pitch. You are a black dot that you drag with your left thumb, at jogging speed only. A sightline cone shows what you can see. Anything behind bodies or far away is greyed and smudged.
- **0:11** A defender clips Rovers' winger. Your right thumb makes the calls: tap to whistle, press and hold for advantage. You hold, and a 5-second ring starts filling around the ball.
- **0:14** The winger keeps the attack going. You release and the ring locks green. A marker stays over the defender, City #4.
- **0:22** The ball goes out for a throw-in. You swipe up on #4 to show a yellow card. The away end's noise bar spikes red and City's captain jogs over. Two options appear: "Last warning" or "Walk away."

**4. Central decision and how the game responds:** For each incident you choose to whistle, play advantage or let it go. You also choose where to stand, and that decides what you can see. Each team keeps a running estimate of where your line is. Let three late tackles go and both sides start tackling harder. Whistle every contact and players start diving while the crowd's flow meter sags. The game rewards consistency over strictness.

**5. Interesting interaction:** A corner puts 14 players in the box. Behind a cluster, a defender pulls the striker's shirt, but all you see is a grey "?" pulse and a roar from 9,000 people. You have three choices:
- Give a penalty on a guess.
- Wave play on.
- Tap the flag icon to ask your assistant referee. He only saw it if it happened on his side of the box, and that depends on which diagonal you chose at kickoff.

At half-time, pundits replay your three biggest calls from the TV angle. If your penalty was given for a dive, your Integrity score drops. That striker also dives twice as often for the rest of the season, because it worked on you.

**6. Between rounds / reason to return:** You referee a season of fixtures and every club remembers you ("City think you're soft on shirt-pulling"). A veteran captain tests you with a late tackle in the first 10 minutes of every match. Rain brings more sliding tackles, and cup finals bring penalty shootouts. After each match, an assessor marks your five key decisions, and those marks decide whether you're promoted to a higher league. The next fixture is often a team you sent a player off against, and they remember.

**7. Borrowed logic:** Association-football refereeing: the advantage rule (signal, wait, bring play back if the advantage doesn't come), the diagonal positioning system, and the assessor's marking of key incidents.

## F2. Copy of a Copy (strange one)

**1. One sentence:** A rhythm game where whatever you play becomes the song. Missed notes vanish forever and extra taps become new notes, so next time you load the tape you're playing your own last performance.

**2. Assumption challenged:** In most games levels are fixed and mistakes don't last. Here the level is your most recent attempt.

**3. 30 seconds of play:**
- **0:00** You tap the cassette "Harbour Waltz, gen 7" on your shelf. It clicks into a deck and the reels turn.
- **0:03** Notes fall down three lanes and you tap with both thumbs. A note only sounds if you hit it.
- **0:09** You're late on a high note. It stays silent and a strike-through marks the reel, meaning gen 8 won't have it.
- **0:15** An empty beat seems to want something, so you tap the mid lane. A new note sounds, tuned to the chord, and now it's part of the song.
- **0:24** You hit 12 notes in a row inside the tight "clean" window, and the hiss audibly thins.
- **0:30** A J-card slides up: "Gen 8: 81 notes (−4, +1), hiss 3, swing +9 ms." You write a label on it.

**4. Central decision and how the game responds:** On each play you either preserve the song or steer it. Every tape has a family tree. Once a song differs from its original master by more than 40% of its notes, it becomes a named variant with its own shelf slot. The master is never available again, so you only own the latest generation. You get one blank tape per day, and you need one to branch a song into two lines.

**5. Interesting interaction:** Hiss goes up by 1 every generation that isn't mostly clean. At hiss 10, the quiet ghost notes and harmonies (about 15% of a chart) sink under the noise and drop out. The song simplifies itself whether you want it to or not. Suppose your Harbour Waltz lost its chorus at gen 9, and a friend mails you their gen 3, with hiss 1 and the chorus intact. You drag a razor across both tapes at a bar line and splice in 8 bars of theirs. Each tape allows 3 splices, and every splice leaves a permanent tick on the downbeat. Your next play is half you and half them.

**6. Between rounds / reason to return:** Each day brings one new master, a "found tape" from a market stall, plus copies friends have mailed you of their current generations. Some players try to keep one song clean to gen 100, and others push a waltz until nobody would recognize it. A monthly "festival" plays every player's version of one master side by side. The songs are built from your own mistakes and inventions, and today's play decides tomorrow's song.

**7. Borrowed logic:** Cassette dubbing, where each copy is made from the last copy, hiss builds up and edits are made by splicing at a bar line. Combined with folk-song transmission, where a tune survives only as the last person sang it.

## F3. Night Orders

**1. One sentence:** A sailing ship takes weeks to cross an ocean, and you command its deck for one watch of 12 orders. Then you write the log and hand over to a stranger, and you may never learn how the voyage ends.

**2. Assumption challenged:** Progress belongs to the player, and you play your own save to its ending.

**3. 30 seconds of play:**
- **0:00** Eight bells ring: "You have the deck." You see the ship from above, with the barometer at 1004 mb and falling. The last log entry reads: "Topsail split, patched. Glass falling. Watch the bosun. (K.)"
- **0:11** You drag the topsail's reef line down one notch and four crew climb, their stamina bars dropping. That's order 1 of 12.
- **0:16** You turn the wheel one point south, away from the falling barometer. Order 2.
- **0:20** You tap the bosun: "Search his locker" (costs 2 orders) or "Leave it."
- **0:26** You press "Stand the watch," and four hours pass in 40 seconds with bells every half hour. Then you write the log. Course and weather fill in by themselves, and you get 80 characters of remarks plus one concern flag (rigging, crew, water, weather or navigation).

**4. Central decision and how the game responds:** You can spend orders on the next four hours or on a future you won't see. Restowing cargo, for example, costs 6 orders and pays off 10 watches later. Then you decide what to write. The next officer sees only the instruments, the log and your flag. The ship also has a hidden state, like a slow leak or a crewman turning sour. If you notice something and leave it out of the log, it becomes someone else's crisis, and the log shows who flagged it and who didn't.

**5. Interesting interaction:** Getting the ship through a crisis, such as shortening sail in a squall with 3 orders, earns you a standing order. It's one sentence pinned to the log for the next 20 watches. Later officers can break it, but they have to write why. Watch 113 orders "Keep north of 40°S," and the ship sits in calms for six watches. Watch 119 breaks the order, heads south, catches the westerlies and makes 300 miles in two watches. The log shows both entries side by side.

**6. Between rounds / reason to return:** Each day you stand a watch on a different ship in the fleet, each in its own kind of trouble, or you can queue to get back on an old one. A crossing takes about 250 watches. When a ship makes port, everyone who served gets the full log and a chart with their own hours highlighted. If a ship sinks, its log ends with the last officer's entry. You return to see what strangers did with your decisions.

**7. Borrowed logic:** Sailing-ship watchkeeping: the four-hour watch rotation, ship's bells every half hour, the captain's standing orders, and the rule that the deck isn't handed over until the relieving officer has read the log.

## F4. Nilometer (strange one)

**1. One sentence:** Before bed you lay out the fields of a small river kingdom. Overnight, your tossing and turning on the mattress becomes one year of the river's flood.

**2. Assumption challenged:** Play is deliberate and conscious. Here the input is your sleeping body, and the skill is predicting yourself.

**Assume** the phone, lying on the mattress, can record how much you move and when, the way accelerometer sleep trackers do.

**3. 30 seconds of play (split between evening and morning):**
- **Evening:**
  - **0:00** You see a valley in cross-section, with the river and terraces 1 to 5 stepping uphill. Tonight's timeline runs across the top in three seasons: the first third of your sleep is Inundation, the middle is Growing and the last is Harvest.
  - **0:06** You drag barley onto terrace 3 and flax onto terrace 1, where the silt is richest.
  - **0:12** You tap "Sleep," and the phone goes face-down on the mattress.
- **Morning:**
  - **0:20** A stone nilometer staircase fills to the night's peak: 15 cubits, "Security." Your movement graph pours across the valley as a flood wave. Restlessness while settling in reached terrace 3 in Inundation and silted the barley. At 3:12 a.m. two roll-overs sent a flood to terrace 2 in Growing and drowned the flax.
  - **0:30** The scribe tallies: barley 140 sacks, flax 0, tax due 90.

**4. Central decision and how the game responds:** Every evening is a bet on what kind of sleeper you'll be after the late coffee, the hard day or the long run. High terraces are safe but get little silt. Low terraces are rich but exposed. The scribe taxes by flood height, not by what survived, so a wild night can bring both a big tax bill and a ruined crop. A night under 12 cubits is a famine year.

**5. Interesting interaction:** For 20 bricks you can build a basin dike on terrace 3. It holds any water that reaches it for 2 hours after the river falls. Here is one night with barley behind it:
- **12:50 a.m.** A brief stir puts water on terrace 3 for 6 minutes. The dike holds it for 2 hours, which counts as a full irrigation.
- **5:40 a.m.** After a still night, a Harvest-season stir reaches only terrace 2. The result is 220 sacks.

If a second spike comes before a full basin drains, the water stacks and climbs one terrace higher than it otherwise would, and that can reach the village. An isolated sharp jolt enters the chronicle as an omen: "A hippopotamus broke the canal in the fourth hour."

**6. Between rounds / reason to return:**
- One night is one year. Surpluses build monuments, and a pyramid takes 30 good years.
- After seven high floods in a row, the river shifts channel: new terraces open and old ones dry out.
- Each reign is named from its record: "[Your name] the Restless," or "of the Still Waters."
- Every morning brings a result you caused without choosing it, and every evening asks you to predict yourself.

**7. Borrowed logic:** Nile flood-basin farming: crops go into land the flood has silted, basin dikes hold water after the river falls, and the nilometer reading in cubits sets both the harvest forecast and the year's tax.
