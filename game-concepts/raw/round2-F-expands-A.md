# Round 2: F (Wildcard) expands A1–A4

---

## A1. Strung

**1. More expressive interaction: bend the figure.** A locked figure stays live. Rocking a holding finger 1–3 mm tightens or slackens every strand anchored to it and bends those pitches by up to a semitone. That turns Jacob's Ladder's 10 strands into a small harp you play by flexing your fingers. On silk, a rock past 5 mm still drops the loop. You can attach a 10-second "figure song" to any silhouette you send.

**2. Unexpected consequence: same shape, different chord.** A silhouette hides over and under at each crossing, but the pitch shows it. (Possible rule: a strand's pitch depends on its free length and how many crossings pinch it.) Players find three different move sequences that reach Tuesday's "Owl" silhouette. They ring in C major, in A minor and as a sour cluster. People start asking "which Owl did you make?", and solving by ear becomes its own skill. A silhouette from a friend now holds two puzzles: match the outline, then match the sound.

**3. Branch A1-b: Fingerloop**

**One sentence:** Instead of building one figure, you braid an endless cord: eight coloured loops ride on your fingertips, and passing one loop through the others, over and over, grows a patterned braid down the screen.

**30 seconds of play:**
- **0:00** Your fingers (four per hand) each hold a loop: red on the outer fingers, cream in the middle. *(Assume 8 simultaneous touches; small screens use 6.)* The braid's end hangs from a hook at the top. Your left index is empty.
- **0:03** Slide that empty finger under the loops on your left middle and ring fingers (sliding passes under, as in Strung) and touch the right index's loop.
- **0:06** Pull straight back to take it straight, or twist your finger as you pull to take it reversed, which flips which colour shows on top.
- **0:09** The left loops walk down a finger, the right loops shift, and now a right-hand finger is empty.
- **0:11** Spread both hands. The stitch tightens with a soft thrum and 3 mm of braid appears below the hook.
- **0:12–0:30** Repeat from the right: reach, take, walk, spread. By pass 8 a red chevron has started.

**Central decision and how the game responds:** On each pass you choose straight or reversed. The braid shows the result about five passes late, so you work ahead like someone following a knitting chart. Alternating reversals make chevrons, one-sided reversals make a spiral, and a wrong twist leaves a permanent stray stitch.

**Surprise:** Letter braids. Each letter is a 12-pass sequence, so a 40 cm cord can carry "MEET AT THE BRIDGE." One mis-twisted pass and your friend reads "MEET AT THE BRIOGE."

**Between rounds / reason to return:** Each day's braiding adds to one continuous cord that scrolls back to day one. Two friends can braid in tandem with phones side by side, passing loops across the gap to make a 16-loop band. A length can be tied off and sent as a bracelet carrying a hidden motto.

**Borrowed logic:** Medieval fingerloop braiding, including its letter braids. Loops ride on fingers, an empty finger reaches through to take a loop straight or reversed, the other loops walk down to refill, and the hands spread to tighten each pass.

## A2. Gather

**1. More expressive interaction: trail and comb.** While your left thumb keeps rolling, touch a colour rod to the gather with your right finger. A thread winds on as a spiral, with spacing set by roll speed: slow rolling gives tight coils. Drag a hooked pick through the threads, alternating up and down, to pull the coils into a feather pattern. A trail only sinks in above about 900 °C, and below that it sits on the surface as a beaded line. So where you cool shapes the pattern as well as the form.

**2. Unexpected consequence: breath-built creatures.** "Breath expands the hottest, thinnest part" is meant for vessels, but it also lets you build in steps. Reheat a 1 cm spot for 1.5 s, puff, and a bulb rises from the side. Players raise a head, then four stubby legs, then ears, one reheat and puff at a time. The order book asks for bowls, and the shelf fills with blown pigs. Because uneven thickness cracks in the annealer, a pig with thin ears on a thick body becomes an overnight bet.

**3. Branch A2-b: Lehr**

**One sentence:** You never blow glass. You run the annealing kiln for other players' pieces, loading its shelves and drawing one overnight cooling curve with your finger.

**30 seconds of play (evening):**
- **0:00** A crate arrives with 7 pieces from 5 players, each with a thickness map. One is a bowl with a 9 mm base and 2 mm walls.
- **0:06** Drag the pieces onto three shelves. The top shelf sits near the elements and runs 15 °C hotter.
- **0:12** Tilt the phone to hold a vase up to the polariscope, where stress shows as rainbow fringes. Okke's vase already has a bright band at the neck.
- **0:18** Draw the curve on a time-by-temperature graph. Rise to 515 °C, hold your finger still for the soak, then slope down. The graph's axis ends at 07:00.
- **0:28** "Close the door." The display now tracks the kiln in real time.

**Central decision and how the game responds:** One curve has to serve every piece. The 2 mm goblet could drop 150 °C an hour. The 9 mm base needs a 90-minute soak and 30 °C an hour from 515 down to 400 °C. A curve slow enough for the thickest piece won't finish by 07:00, so early-rising owners see "still cooling" and wait. In the morning, each crack pings on your screen and the owner's, and your kiln stamp is on the tag.

**Surprise:** Hold above about 570 °C for 20 minutes and the glass starts slumping. A goblet's bowl ends up bowed 30° to one side. Its owner can remelt it or keep it as "kiln-touched." Some blowers start sending pieces with notes like "sag it 45°, please."

**Between rounds / reason to return:** Crates arrive daily, and your stamp keeps a survival record for each thickness. A commissioned 40 cm vase ties up the kiln for three nights. You're the last hands on other people's work, and every morning brings their pings and their notes.

**Borrowed logic:** Glass annealing. You soak at the annealing point until the thickest section evens out, cool slowly through the strain range, and check for leftover stress under polarized light.

## A3. Belfry

**1. More expressive interaction: call changes.** In this mode, one thumb rings and the other calls. Tap two numbers in the margin, such as "5 to 3," and those bells swap at the next handstroke. You walk the band through named rows like Queens (135246), Kings (531246) and Tittums (142536) and hold any row as long as you like. Calls that move your own bell mean you pull a stroke ahead of your own command.

**2. Unexpected consequence: ghosts that outlive their ringers.** A ghost bell can't hear a live call, so a band with ghosts has to ring exactly the touch each ghost recorded. Conductors start keeping libraries of matching recordings ("we need a 3rd who rang the 120 of Plain Bob with bobs at leads 2 and 4"). When a player stops playing, their ghost keeps ringing in other people's towers for months. Bands then do what real ringers do for a lost ringer and ring a half-muffled quarter peal. Their ghost rings its usual bell, still clipping the backstroke at 5ths the way it always did.

**3. Branch A3-b: First Peal**

**One sentence:** Instead of ringing a method, you invent one. You set which bells stay put in each row on a grid while a computer band rings it, and if it comes round true it's named after you and joins the library Belfry players ring.

**30 seconds of play:**
- **0:00** A six-column grid starts on rounds, 123456. Today's brief reads: "12-row lead; the treble plain-hunts; no bell lies in 6ths more than twice."
- **0:04** For the next row, tap columns to "make places," meaning those bells stay put. Every other bell swaps with its neighbour. Choices that leave an odd bell out grey themselves out.
- **0:09** Each bell's path draws as a coloured line, and the band rings every row as you commit it.
- **0:16** Row 9 matches row 3. A flat clang sounds and both rows flash red: false. You change row 8 from "all swap" to "make 1st and 4ths," and every row is unique again.
- **0:26** You close the lead. The band rings the whole plain course while the lines weave.

**Central decision and how the game responds:** You decide which places to make so that rows never repeat and every working bell takes its turn at every piece of work. The game rings the full plain course, marks any false row and shows each bell's path side by side.

**Surprise:** A lead can look perfect and still end with bells 2 and 3 swapped while 4, 5 and 6 rotate. The course then runs 6 leads (72 rows), and bells 2 and 3 only ever trade two pieces of work between them while 4–6 share the other three. The method is legal but differential, and it's filed in its own library.

**Between rounds / reason to return:** Each day brings a new brief. Your true methods enter the shared library, where you can hear strangers ring something you invented, under your name.

**Borrowed logic:** Change-ringing composition. It uses place notation, truth (no row may repeat), the lead head deciding how bells rotate through the work, and the custom that a new method is named by the band that first rings it.

## A4. Pocket Tumbler

**1. More expressive interaction: roll or rattle.** How you carry the phone becomes a choice. Carried upright in a pocket, each step makes the stones tumble over each other, a rotary action that rounds them into pebbles. Carried flat in a bag or lying on a car seat, motion becomes vibration. Stones jitter in place, their corners survive and their faces polish flat. (Possible rule: each motion counts toward the stone's roll % or rattle % depending on orientation.) Carry a jasper slab flat all week for a crisp, faceted block, or switch it upright halfway through the coarse stage to round off only the sharpest corners.

**2. Unexpected consequence: guilds by lifestyle.** Every player's daily life is a different machine. A runner gets through coarse grit in two days but bruises anything in polish. A 90-minute driving commute gives beautiful polish and almost no coarse grind. Stones can ride in friends' barrels, so households split stages: the runner takes coarse, the commuter takes polish. An agate's card reads "coarse: Dana's 10K training; polish: Sam's Line 4 bus," and people start choosing their route home by which stage a friend's stone is in.

**3. Branch A4-b: Black Sand**

**One sentence:** You pan a creek for gold and rough gems by shaking, tipping and swirling the phone like a prospector's pan, and the creek only restocks when it actually rains where you live.

*Assume the game can read local weather.*

**30 seconds of play:**
- **0:00** A top-down creek shows a bend, a big boulder and a crack in the exposed bedrock. You tap the inside of the bend, and your pan scoops gravel there.
- **0:05** Hold the phone flat and shake it side to side. Haptics rattle, heavy grains sink, and pale gravel rises to the top.
- **0:10** Tip the phone forward 15° and swirl in small circles. Each swirl washes a thin layer of light gravel over the lip. Tip too steeply and the bottom layer goes too.
- **0:18** Fingertip out the big rocks, keeping a lentil-sized garnet. You're down to a teaspoon of black sand. Tilt back and swish gently. The sand fans out into a tail, and three gold flecks glint at its head. Tap them into the vial.

**Central decision and how the game responds:** You choose where to dig and how hard to wash. Gold settles where the water slows: inside bends, behind boulders and in bedrock cracks. Fast washing sends fine gold over the lip, and you only learn the loss at the end ("0.04 g washed out").

**Surprise:** Most pans give a few flecks. The bedrock crack works like a natural riffle that has been filling for years. Drag a finger along it to scrape it out, and one pan yields 0.6 g of gold and a 3 mm sapphire. After that the crack stays empty until the next heavy rain.

**Between rounds / reason to return:** Real rain where you live reshapes the creek overnight, with new gravel bars and refilled cracks. A dry spell means re-working old tailings for fine gold. Rough gems can go into a Pocket Tumbler barrel, and a thunderstorm warning becomes good news.

**Borrowed logic:** Gold panning and placer prospecting. Heavy material settles under shaking, light material washes off the lip in thin layers, and floods restock gold where water slows.
