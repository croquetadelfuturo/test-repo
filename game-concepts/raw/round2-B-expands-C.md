# Round 2: B (Information designer) expands C (Systems designer)

## C1. Point of Balance

### More expressive interaction: compose your own whistles
Before a trial, you draw each command as a whistle on a pitch strip, and the dog learns whatever you draw. A one-note whistle arrives in 0.2 s. A three-note whistle takes 0.6 s but is harder to mistake. In a gale, similar whistles blur. If your "come-bye" and "walk up" both start low and rise, the dog mishears 40% of the time. Drawing a whistle slowly asks for a gentle, wide flank.

### Unexpected consequence
Gales delay whistles by 0.5 s, so a windy week teaches handlers to whistle early. The dog picks up your habits, so it learns to expect early whistles too. On the next calm day it starts flanking before the whistle ends. On the drive that looks like mind-reading. At the shed it is a disaster, because the dog commits before you've seen which way the lead ewe turns. Players keep separate "wind dogs" and "calm dogs", and a dog lent to a friend comes back with the friend's timing.

### Branch C1-b: Look Back
**1. One sentence:** Two players run one trial: the handler sees the whole field but can only whistle, while the dog sees from the grass, close enough to read each sheep's fear, and decides what each whistle means. A solo player plays the dog.

**2. 30 seconds of play**
- The dog sees from the grass. Gorse blocks the view, and pinned-back ears mean a sheep will bolt. The handler has C1's top-down view and four whistles, with no sign of fear.
- The handler sees the flock drift toward clover and whistles "come-bye" (circle clockwise).
- The dog sees the lead ewe's ears pinned on that side and loops 6 m wider than asked.
- The handler sees the dog over-flank. Is it disobeying, or does it know something? Whistle "lie down", or stay silent.

**3. Central decision and how the game responds:** The dog chooses whether to obey literally or interpret. The handler chooses which command carries their intent, and when silence means trust. After the judge scores the run, a replay shows both screens side by side: "Your dog went wide because the ewe at 4 m had pinned ears."

**4. Surprise:** Some courses hide a sixth sheep in a hollow, where only the dog can pick up her scent. To fetch her, the dog has to leave the flock mid-fetch without being asked. The handler sees the dog bolt sideways and can whistle it back, losing the sixth sheep's 10 points, or wait. Pairs invent their own dialect: two quick "lie down" whistles appear in no rulebook, but they can come to mean "I know, go and look."

**5. What changes between rounds / reason to return:** Roles swap and courses add blind spots. AI handlers have temperaments (one over-whistles, one goes quiet when nervous). A pair's dialect carries over, and the replay archive shows how it formed.

**6. Borrowed logic:** Sheepdog trials: knowledge split between a handler fixed at the post and a dog close to the sheep, the "look back" command for missed sheep, and the tradition that a good dog sometimes overrides a wrong whistle.

## C2. Long Fire

### More expressive interaction: straw and shells
While packing, draw on a pot with a finger to wrap it in rice straw: a spiral, bands, an initial. Wherever straw touched, the firing leaves red-orange lines. Shells pressed under a pot's foot leave pale crescent scars. The drawing is a request, not a print. Sheltered behind another pot, the lines come out crisp. In the ash-heavy front row, green glaze buries them, and a spiral half-facing the firebox comes out half drowned.

### Unexpected consequence
Shelf drips carry over between firings and buyers ask for particular marks, so players sacrifice pots on purpose. A cheap plate tilted 12° in firing 1 leaves a green drip on shelf B4, ready for a commissioned tea bowl in firing 2. Because unloading waits until the next session, players also post their loading diagram and a prediction ("three stripes, one fused plate") before they unbrick, and friends post theirs. The night before unloading becomes the social part of the game.

### Branch C2-b: Night Shift
**1. One sentence:** Six players share one wood firing that lasts five real days. Each takes short stoking shifts, sees the kiln only through spy holes, and passes on what they know in a logbook with a fixed vocabulary.

**2. 30 seconds of play**
- It is 07:10, hour 58 of 120. The previous stoker, Tomas, left this log card: "H54 · back lagging · DON'T side-stoke yet · back cone 10 down."
- Through spy hole 4 at the back, you see three test cones (9, 10, 11) that bend as they reach their heat. Cone 9 is still standing, which contradicts Tomas. Through hole 3 in the middle, cone 10 is bent over. Maybe that's what he saw.
- Your shift is four kiln-hours, about three minutes. Stoke the front in rhythm while watching the smoke, and at minute two open the side port anyway.
- Write your log entry with up to 20 word tiles: "H62 · back cone 9 STANDING · hole 4 · side-stoked · Tomas = hole 3?"

**3. Central decision and how the game responds:** You decide whether to follow the crew's plan or override it, and what to tell, in 20 tiles, a person you'll never speak to. Each crew member also has one pot in the kiln, placed at random, so whoever's pot sits at the back is tempted to push heat there. The next stoker sees only your words and the spy holes.

**4. Surprise:** At unloading, each pot has a ribbon showing who was stoking when its ash fell. The back row came out chalky, and its ribbons show 12 underfired hours, from H54 to H66. They trace back to Tomas's "back cone 10 down", which he wrote after looking through hole 3. Three stokers believed him, and you were the first to check.

**5. What changes between rounds / reason to return:** Each firing brings a new crew, kiln and wood. You sign up for shifts at real hours, nights included. The next stoker rates your entries as useful or unclear, and new word tiles unlock as you learn to read the cones.

**6. Borrowed logic:** Communal wood firings: stoking shifts, a firing log passed from crew to crew, and test cones read through spy holes.

## C3. Water Temple

### More expressive interaction: marriage threads
Every five years, the priest can tie a thread between two blocks that aren't neighbours, standing for a marriage or a pilgrimage route. For five years, a tied block also looks at its partner's harvest when choosing whom to copy. Tie block 2 to lakeside block 8, and in year 6 block 2 copies it, skipping blocks 3–7. The priest shapes who sees whose results.

### Unexpected consequence
Harvests depend on a block's position as well as its calendar. Block 1 gets the river first and nearly always does well, so its calendar spreads downhill on position, not merit: a valley superstition. In a drought year, block 2 copies block 1's odd late calendar and fails on less water. The priest's best move can be a firebreak: spending a seal to make one block plant badly on purpose, so no one copies it and the bad calendar stops there for a year or two. One possible rule: the sacrificed block ignores the next decree.

### Branch C3-b: Kliwon
**1. One sentence:** The priest writes each block's decree as a sentence in a calendar of interlocking weeks. Farmers copy the sentence, not the dates, so the same words can mean different days in different places.

**2. 30 seconds of play**
- Three rings turn: 3-, 5- and 7-day weeks, with a glyph for each day. Gold sparks mark named coincidences, like the 5-day week's Kliwon landing on a Tuesday (every 35 days).
- In block 1's slot, drag tiles to make: "Flood on the [first] [Kliwon] after [the rains]." The 210-day strip below marks day 12.
- In block 2's slot: "Flood on the [first] [Kliwon] after [the block above floods]." This sentence is relative.
- Run a few years. The blocks below copy block 2's sentence, and floods ripple down the strip on days 12, 17, 22…

**3. Central decision and how the game responds:** You choose between absolute and relative wording, and which rhythm to anchor to. Once copied, absolute sentences make blocks act on the same day: pests starve, water strains. Relative ones stagger automatically: water is shared, pests are relayed. The strip redraws with water use as a colour band and pests as specks.

**4. Surprise:** By year 10, blocks 1–8 flood five days apart, from day 12 to day 47. You change one word in block 1, "first" to "third". Block 1 moves to day 22, and blocks 2–5 slide 10 days with it. But block 6 had copied "the first Tuesday-Kliwon after the block above floods", which comes only every 35 days. It only looked chained because day 37 fell five days after block 5. With block 5 now on day 42, block 6 waits until day 72, and blocks 7–8 follow it five weeks late, into the dry months. Their harvests fall to 22.

**5. What changes between rounds / reason to return:** Years and valleys vary as in C3, and a 6-day week unlocks. Farmers sometimes drop a word when copying, so "after the block above floods" becomes "after floods". The valley's history reads as a family tree of sentences.

**6. Borrowed logic:** The Balinese pawukon calendar, whose concurrent weeks mark ritual days where they coincide, used as a grammar for subak water scheduling.

## C4. Caller

### More expressive interaction: how much to say
A call can be full ("Robins chain across, courtesy turn"), landing 4 beats early and teaching the figure; short ("Chain"), landing 2 beats early; or silent. Each dancer's hidden memory of the dance grows with every correct time through, shown only by how confidently their hat bobs. When you go silent, the band plays a livelier variation, but dancers with weak memory drift and collide. Some callers talk throughout; others drop out early and return with one word to rescue a lost couple.

### Unexpected consequence
C4's frozen-counter sequence, which keeps Red swinging between the same two neighbours, becomes a matchmaking tool. When Red and Green keep smiling at each other, the caller can choose that sequence so they meet every other time through for ten minutes. A sequence that moves couples two places at a time keeps feuding couples apart. Because couples at the ends sit out, a sequence that progresses unevenly can rest a tired Purple without anyone noticing. The dancers never see that who met whom was a choice.

### Branch C4-b: The Collector
**1. One sentence:** From a balcony, you watch a village perform a dance you've never seen and write it down as calls. From the moving hats you work out which figures it contains, which couples are lost, and where this village's version differs from your book.

**2. 30 seconds of play**
- From above, eight couples dance at full speed, and you can't pause. Below are C4's 64-beat strip and figure cards.
- Beats 1–16: neighbours pair up and spin for a long time. Drag Swing neighbour (16).
- Beats 17–24: the robins cross, then turn with partners, a hand on the back: a chain, not a pass through. Drag Robins chain.
- Ghost dancers overlay the real ones, dancing your transcription; matches vanish, mismatches glow. Yellow, third from the top, glows: their robin never crossed.

**3. Central decision and how the game responds:** For each mismatch, you decide whether it is part of the dance or a mistake. You get eight times through, plus one blurred replay of the last 16 beats each time. Marking Yellow "lost" tells the ghosts to ignore them. At the end, an old dancer reads your notation back and dances it with a beginners' set.

**4. Surprise:** By time through 4, your transcription matches six couples, but Yellow and Orange glow every time. Yellow is plainly lost. Orange dances every figure correctly but with the wrong people. Tracing their hats back shows that Orange came out on the wrong line at the end of time through 2. Since a couple's line sets its direction, Orange has been moving up while the other odd-numbered couples move down. Mark them "wrong direction" instead of "lost", and the ghosts line up with everyone except Yellow.

**5. What changes between rounds / reason to return:** Villages bring their own traditions (squares, crooked tunes). Fog can hide half the hall, and tempos change. Collected dances become callable in C4. The same dance collected in three villages differs slightly, and the differences hint at which village taught which.

**6. Borrowed logic:** Folk dance collecting: writing down a dance by watching it danced, and telling the dance itself apart from dancers' mistakes and local variations.
