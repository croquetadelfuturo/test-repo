# E expands F: Round 2

## F1. Whistle

**1. More expressive interaction: the quiet word.** While play is live, drag from your dot to a player within 15 m and choose one of five words: "Careful", "Hands", "Get up", "Ten yards" or "Last one". City #4 lets go of the shirt within half a second. You get 12 words per half. A word's weight comes from your history: if "Last one" has been followed by a card three times out of four, players obey it, and if it never has, they ignore it.

**2. Unexpected consequence: the league learns your blind spots.** Teams only know what you can see from where you stand. A referee who always runs the left diagonal leaves the far post to nobody, and over a season the clubs move their dark arts there. By matchday 20, City take every corner to the far post, where their #5 stands on the keeper's foot behind a teammate. The league's playing style becomes a portrait of where you run. Switch diagonals and you catch fouls teams thought were safe.

**3. Branch F1-b: Jumpers for Goalposts**

*One sentence.* You're the oldest kid at a park kickabout with no rulebook. You settle arguments and approve the rules the kids invent mid-game, and you score by how long the game lasts before everyone goes home.

*30 seconds.*
- **0:00** 6:40 p.m. Ten kids, jumpers for posts, no crossbar.
- **0:07** A shot flies over the left jumper at about head height. Two kids sprint to you: "It was under the bar!" / "There IS no bar!" From where you stood it looked like 1.7 m. Your notebook says *Tues: 1.9 m, no goal.* You tap **Goal**, and a new line appears in your handwriting.
- **0:16** Mia, 8, holds up a card: *Rush goalie: anyone can go in goal.* You swipe right, and both keepers sprint upfield.
- **0:25** Someone's mum shouts "Tea in five!" and a *Next goal wins* card appears. Swipe to allow it or refuse.

*Central decision.* You rule on every dispute and every proposed rule. Each kid has a fairness meter. If you contradict the notebook, someone quotes it back: "You said 1.9 was over!" When a kid's meter empties, they take their jumper home, and that jumper was a goalpost. The evening's score is minutes played × kids still there at dark.

*Surprise.* You allow Dev's rule, "three corners make a penalty." Within 90 seconds the Blues stop shooting and start blasting the ball off Red shins. Three corners later they have a penalty and a 2–1 lead, and every Red kid's meter drops by 20. Repeal it as a "baby rule" and Dev's meter halves. Accept Jas's patch, "off your own shin doesn't count," and one rule now fills three notebook lines.

*Between rounds / return.* Each evening adds lines to the street's handwritten constitution. Kids grow up: Mia proposes sharper rules at 11, and Dev moves away. Sometimes another street's kids turn up with their own notebook, and the two must be merged before kickoff.

*Borrowed logic:* Street football conventions (no crossbar, rush goalie, three corners a penalty, next goal wins), invented mid-game and settled by argument and remembered precedent.

## F2. Copy of a Copy (strange one)

**1. More expressive interaction: touch weight.** How hard you press sets each note's volume in the next generation. A firm press with your thumb flat puts the note on the tape loud. A light brush makes it a ghost note, which the hiss will swallow at hiss 10. Players can now phrase a song and choose which notes to protect. A soft note is one you've agreed to let go of in a few generations.

**2. Unexpected consequence: songs wear into the shape of your thumbs.** The notes you tend to miss vanish, and the notes you add land wherever your thumbs naturally fall. After 30 generations, a song is a record of your hands. Syncopations go first, your weaker thumb's lane thins, and your habit of tapping the "and" before beat 1 hardens into a hook. Mailed tapes carry the accent, so friends can name the player before reading the label: "This is a Jonah. He always adds a pickup into bar 17."

**3. Branch F2-b: Stemma**

*One sentence.* A song's master is lost and only its descendants survive. You compare battered copies bar by bar, work out which tape was copied from which, and rebuild the original.

*30 seconds.*
- **0:00** Five "Harbour Waltz" cassettes sit on a desk, each labelled with where it was found (a Lisbon stall, Gran's attic, a busker...). Tap bar 12 and five strips stack up, one per tape. Tap one to hear it.
- **0:08** Tapes 1, 3 and 4 lack the high E on beat 2. You loop them together as a *shared loss*, and in the tree panel they hang from one dotted parent: a copy that dropped the note.
- **0:17** In bar 13, tapes 4 and 5 share an extra out-of-key note, which contradicts your tree. A dashed red line appears, labelled *splice?*
- **0:25** You take tape 2's reading for bar 12, high E kept, and the rebuilt master gains a bar.

*Central decision.* For each bar, choose which reading to trust and the family tree that justifies it. Once tapes 1, 3 and 4 share a parent, their agreement counts as one witness rather than three, and their strips merge on screen. At the end the game plays the true master over your reconstruction and lights up every bar you got right.

*Surprise.* Tape 2 is the cleanest, at hiss 1 with every note on the grid. It's a later re-recording by someone who tidied the song up. The master had a lilt, with beat 2 arriving 30 ms early in every bar, and only tape 4 preserves it, the hissiest on the desk. Copyists smooth things out and rarely invent awkwardness, so you learn to trust the stranger reading.

*Between rounds / return.* One new case arrives each day, with 3–9 surviving copies. Later cases mix lineages through splices, or include a tape copied from another surviving tape, which adds nothing as evidence. Restored masters go on a shelf and can be loaded into Copy of a Copy as gen 0, so decay begins again from your reconstruction.

*Borrowed logic:* Textual criticism (stemmatics): grouping manuscripts by shared errors, counting descendants of one copy as a single witness, and preferring the harder reading.

## F3. Night Orders

**1. More expressive interaction: the margin sketch.** Beside the 80 characters, the log has a margin where you can draw three pencil strokes with your finger. It might be an arrow at the forecastle, a squiggle of cloud in the west, or a ring around the bosun's name. The strokes have no defined meaning, and the next officer sees them exactly as drawn. Over a crossing, the margins become a second log that only half-means things.

**2. Unexpected consequence: the warning makes the crisis.** On watch 61, K. writes "Watch the bosun" on a hunch. Each of the next six officers spends 2 orders searching his locker and finds nothing. Each search costs the bosun 5 loyalty, and by watch 70 he's leading a go-slow. The mutiny exists because the log warned about it. The record credits K. with flagging it first, and later officers can't tell whether K. was perceptive or the cause.

**3. Branch F3-b: Dead Reckoning**

*One sentence.* Under overcast skies, a ship's position is a pencil line that each navigator extends from the last one's guess. You stand one watch as navigator, measure speed and heading by hand, and hand on a position nobody can check.

*30 seconds.*
- **0:00** Two bells. You swipe to throw the chip log, and the knotted line runs out under your thumb. You tap to turn the 28-second sandglass and tap again when it empties. Six knots and a bit, so you call it 6.5.
- **0:10** On the traverse board, a peg marks each half-hour of the last watch's heading. Most point WSW, two point W, and one is crooked.
- **0:18** At the chart table, the last line ends at *Noon? (R.)*. You lay the ruler from it, angle it for one point of leeway, and draw 26 miles.
- **0:27** You log the position and sign it. The ship's true position is somewhere else, and nobody can see where.

*Central decision.* Every input carries error. You judge when the sand has really run out, how much leeway and current to allow, and whether to trust R.'s circle. The game never shows the truth. It answers with evidence: sea colour, birds, what the lead brings up.

*Surprise.* You spend your one extra action heaving the lead. It finds bottom at 40 fathoms, and the tallow comes up with fine grey sand and black specks. The chart marks *fne gy S bk sp* in two places 70 miles apart: one near your line, one to the north. You move the position north and write "Moved per sounding." Eighteen watches later the lookout sights the headland exactly where your northern position predicted.

*Between rounds / return.* You navigate one watch a day, each on a different ship. On a clear noon, a sun sight reveals the true track against everyone's pencil, with your 26 miles and their error highlighted. At landfall every navigator receives the chart, covered in 200 strangers' overlapping lines.

*Borrowed logic:* Pre-satellite dead reckoning: the chip log timed by a sandglass, the traverse board, judgment on leeway and current, and the armed lead that samples the seabed to check position against the chart.

## F4. Nilometer (strange one)

**1. More expressive interaction: canals drawn with a finger.** Each evening you can draw a canal from the riverbank at any height to any terrace. Its intake height decides when it fills. A canal cut at 14 cubits runs only on restless nights, while one cut at 11 runs almost every night. Water runs to the canal's far end, so a restless sleeper can send their worst hours to a dry high orchard. Each valley becomes a drawing of how its owner believes they sleep.

**2. Unexpected consequence: other bodies in the bed.** The phone can't tell whose movement it feels. A partner who rolls over at 2 a.m. floods terrace 2. A cat that lands on the bed at 4:10 every morning becomes the same hippopotamus breaking the same canal, night after night. Players plant around them, with a dike where "the hippo" always strikes. The chronicle becomes a household diary, and couples negotiate: "Can you take the sofa? It's harvest."

**3. Branch F4-b: Two Rivers**

*One sentence.* Two people sleeping apart (a long-distance couple, siblings in different cities) are each one river of a shared plain. Every morning, the first to wake decides whose night waters whose fields.

*Assume* each phone records its sleeper's movement as in F4, and the plain runs on one shared clock.

*30 seconds.*
- **Evening.** Noor in Glasgow plants barley on her bank, and Sami in Toronto plants wheat on his. Six basins lie between the rivers, joined by gates.
- **07:00 Glasgow.** Noor wakes from a 17-cubit night, with water pressing on gates 2 and 4. In Toronto it's 2 a.m., so Sami's river is only half-drawn and his wheat is dry.
- **07:05** She drags gates 2 and 4 open, and her flood crosses into his basins. She presses one of 12 seals into a clay tablet ("rough night", "owe you", a reed, a fish).
- **07:00 Toronto.** Sami wakes to watered wheat and the tablet, and seals "owe you" back.

*Central decision.* The first to wake gives water or holds it back, and the second accepts it or drains it to the marsh. Water saves today's crop but carries salt. Each transfer adds 1 salt to the receiving basin, wheat fails at salt 5 and barley at 8, and three fallow nights remove 2.

*Surprise: the Deluge.* Their nights overlap from 04:00 to 07:00 Glasgow time. If both peaks pass 16 cubits within the same 2-hour window, the rivers meet and every basin floods. This happens the night before Noor flies to Toronto, when neither sleeps. Every crop is lost, and the tablet writes itself: *In the year of the flight, the two rivers met.* But the Deluge resets salt to 0 and leaves +3 silt everywhere, so the following year is the plain's richest.

*Between rounds / return.* Each shared night is a year. When the two sleep in the same bed during a visit, the rivers flood in step, and only then can a new canal be dug between the banks. Over months the plain shows where wheat gave way to barley: a map of who carried whom.

*Borrowed logic:* Mesopotamian irrigation between the Tigris and Euphrates: shared canals and gates between neighbours, water agreements pressed into clay, and salt build-up that pushed farmers from wheat to barley.
