# D (Time designer) expands E1–E4

---

## E1. Pace Notes

**1. More expressive interaction: glyph lines.**
During the recce, Sam can long-press a line and draw a corner with a finger instead of using the palette, for example a hairpin with a bump on its exit. Each glyph uses 2 of the 40 lines, with a maximum of 3 per stage. During the stage Sam sees only the drawing and has to find words on the spot ("the elbow with the hiccup"). The calibration strip compares Priya's speed through glyph corners with her speed through numbered ones. Over time glyphs pick up stable spoken names, and notes can range from strict code to private hieroglyph.

**2. Unexpected consequence: silence becomes a note, and the numbers start describing the driver.**
With only 40 lines, co-drivers leave out the easy bends, and drivers learn that "nothing said" means "flat." On Swap Notes stages, another crew's silence may mean "ran out of lines," and the driver goes flat into an unannounced 3. "Minus one" drifts the same way. Sam uses it on a dry road because Priya is driving angry after a slide, so the number is managing her mood rather than describing the gravel. Notebooks start to read like portraits of the driver.

**3. Branch: E1-b. Tape Run**
*One sentence:* The co-driver records one unbroken spoken take of the notes while watching the stage at a speed they choose, and later the driver races to that tape, which can't be paused or rewound.

*30 seconds of play:* At 23:10 in Toronto, Sam sets the recce to 110% of par and taps record. The road rolls by, and Sam calls it aloud in one 2:09 take with no do-overs. *(Assume the phone records voice and marks where each call falls on the road.)* At 07:40 the next morning in London, Priya launches into fog, and the tape's "Go" fires at the same instant. At 0:40, "right three tightens" arrives with the corner still 60 m off. A bar reads **tape +1.8 s**.

*Central decision and response:* Sam decides what tempo to promise, and Priya decides whether to chase the tape. If she falls more than 3 s behind, the calls stack up in her ear. If she gets ahead, she reaches corners before Sam's voice and drives them in silence. The result screen shows the tape's trace beside the run's, and any section driven within 0.5 s of the tape earns a gold **in sync** stripe.

*Surprise:* Sam coughs at 1:12 on the take, and the cough stays on the tape. Priya learns it comes right before the long right, so it works as a note. When she beats the tape by two seconds, the tape has become the slower partner, and the next week Sam records at 118%.

*Between rounds / reason to return:* There are new stages weekly and roles swap. Tapes stay on the pair's shelf, so a driver can re-race an old one or race a top crew's tape. The pair never needs to be awake at the same time. A voice is waiting, set to someone's idea of how fast you can go.

*Borrowed logic:* Studio overdubbing to a guide track: the first performer fixes the tempo, and everyone who records later plays inside it.

## E2. Kasen

**1. More expressive interaction: the hinge pin.**
Before sending, the sender drags a pin from a spot in the friend's photo (the red sock) to a spot in their own (the postbox slot), and the scroll draws a fine thread between them. The sender can also leave the pin unplaced as a **hidden hinge**. If the receiver places it within a thumb's width of the sender's private choice, both photos get a knot mark. Some players hinge on colour, some on shape, some on a gesture, and across 36 verses the threads show what kind of noticer each friend is.

**2. Unexpected consequence: the moon sets the tempo.**
Moon seats are fixed and the moon keeps its own calendar, so pairs count ahead: "If we each take a day, 13 lands on the new moon." Mara holds verse 12 for three days, and Jonah answers verse 11 within an hour, so that 13 falls on a full moon. The chain's pace starts following the sky. Blossom seats do something similar. An October chain in Lisbon has no blossom, so players invite a friend in Melbourne for verses 17 and 35, and spring and autumn end up on the same scroll.

**3. Branch: E2-b. Swap Roll**
*One sentence:* Two distant friends share one 36-exposure virtual roll. One shoots every frame, the roll "ships," the other shoots over each frame blind, and nobody sees anything until it's developed.

*30 seconds of play:* Jonah is on frame 19. He puts a streetlamp in the top-left corner against black sky and keeps the bottom half dark on purpose. *Click.* As a frame note, he drags a "lamp" chip into the top-left of a grey rectangle and adds "lower half is yours." A week later in Lisbon, Mara's frame 19 shows only that note. She shoots her cat's candlelit face low in the frame, without seeing the result.

*Central decision and response:* The first shooter decides how much dark space to leave and how honest the notes are. The second decides where her subject goes and how to answer the word. The game stays silent until development, 24 hours after the last frame ("at the lab"), when the contact sheet arrives.

*Surprise:* Light adds, as on film. Black areas are space for the other exposure, and bright on bright burns to white. Frame 19: the lamp lands exactly in the cat's eye. Frame 22: noon beach plus sunny kitchen comes out almost blank, with a ghost of a towel. Frame 13: a tiny moon sits beside Gran's face like an earring.

*Between rounds / reason to return:* Shooting order swaps each roll. Film stocks change the rules (black-and-white, vivid slide, expired film with colour shifts). There's always a frame count to fill or a development day coming.

*Borrowed logic:* Photographers' film swaps: shoot a roll, rewind it, post it to a friend who exposes over it blind, then develop it and see both exposures at once.

## E3. Long Hum (strange one)

**1. More expressive interaction: leading a bend.**
Any voice can slide its pitch by as much as two semitones. If another voice follows within 2 s and stays within a semitone, the two strands **braid**, twisting together on screen and in the cloth row. Gran leads a slow rise, Theo follows, and Ash stubbornly holds the floor note, leaving one straight strand beside the braid. The hum becomes a wordless conversation about who leads, and the cloth keeps a record of it.

**2. Unexpected consequence: the thread becomes an evening.**
With three or more voices breathing at staggered moments, nothing ends the thread except life. Families stop treating it as a challenge and leave it running. Gran hums while she knits, Theo over the washing-up, and Ash between bites of toast. A thread runs to 2:41:07, so that night's cloth row is a long stripe. The needles become the only events, like calling everyone to the table. The snap stops being a failure and becomes the real goodnight: the moment the last two voices both decide to stop.

**3. Branch: E3-b. Nine O'Clock Hum**
*One sentence:* One hum travels west around the world with nine o'clock in the evening. At their own 21:00, each player hears only the last three seconds of the previous breath and adds one breath, starting on the note it ended on.

*Assumption:* the phone detects voicing and pitch, and the gaps between players are cut out when the thread is stitched.

*30 seconds of play:* At 21:00 in Cork, Gran's phone chimes once. In one earbud she hears three seconds of a stranger in Berlin, recorded an hour ago, dropping to a low D. Gran enters on D, holds for 14 seconds, rises to F, and fades. Her strand joins a thread that has circled 41% of a small globe.

*Central decision and response:* Gran decides how long to hold and where to leave the pitch for a stranger. An entry within a semitone of the tail makes a smooth splice, and a 1–3 semitone jump ties a knot. A bigger jump snaps the thread, and a new one starts from that voice, so a thread's length is how far round the world it got.

*Surprise:* The thread Gran left on F at 21:14 on Monday comes back to Cork at 21:00 on Tuesday as a low G-flat. Tapping **trace** plays the drift as a 40-second line: it rose in Lima, sank in Manila, and wobbled across a Pacific where only three people picked it up. She can play the whole 31-minute lap and find her 14 seconds between a Berlin teenager and a low voice in Reykjavík.

*Between rounds / reason to return:* There's a new lap every day. A **family thread** passes only among relatives, from Ash's 21:00 in Melbourne to Gran, Theo and Ruth eleven hours later. You come back at nine for a tail from the east, and the next night to hear what became of your note.

*Borrowed logic:* Exquisite corpse: each contributor sees only the edge the previous person left and continues from it.

## E4. You Cut, I Choose

**1. More expressive interaction: show your working.**
In async play, the cutter's drag path is recorded and replayed to the chooser at real speed, including the key slid left, then right, then left again, and the five-second hover over the cassette. A cutter can pay 1 point to seal a **clean cut** that shows only the result. Or they can let the hesitations show, or fake some: hovering over junk and gliding past the thing they actually want. The cut becomes a small piece of theatre, standing in for the face you can't see across the table.

**2. Unexpected consequence: collusion, then an endgame cliff.**
Dessert doubles both scores, so siblings invent codes ("my card's things go on the left"), and for six rounds the game is cooperative. Then round 7 comes with dessert already out of reach, so spite costs nothing. End-of-game replays show **Spite ×2** clustered in the final rounds of games that had been entirely gifts. Over time the ledger records *when* each sibling stops being kind. The brass key creates custody drama as well: after a long streak with one sibling, the other gives up whole rounds just to break it.

**3. Branch: E4-b. Slow Knife**
*One sentence:* A knife crawls along a table of family things from breakfast to bedtime. Any sibling can call **Stop** to take everything it has passed and leave the table, and the knife keeps moving for the rest.

*30 seconds of play:* At 13:12, Leo opens the app on his lunch break. Twenty-four items sit in a row, and the knife is between items 9 and 10, passing roughly one item every 35 minutes from 08:00 to 22:00. Items behind the knife glow. Items ahead are face-down boxes until 30 minutes before the knife arrives. Leo's card is "Music." The radio (item 4) is behind the knife, and the harmonica (item 15) is still a box. Three chairs are still occupied. He closes the app.

*Central decision and response:* Stop now, or wait for more and risk someone stopping first and taking it all. A stop splits the table at the knife, and the knife carries on for whoever remains. At 22:00 the last sibling gets everything that's left. Others only learn of a stop when they next look and see an empty chair.

*Surprise:* Grandad's medals turn face-up at 14:15, and the knife will pass them at 14:45. Ana is in a meeting until 16:00. Cass opens the app at 14:52 at a petrol station and stops, taking items 1–12: the medals, and also Leo's radio, which isn't on her card at all. At 21:50 Leo stops on item 23, leaving Ana a single box: the locked trunk, hers to open next game.

*Between rounds / reason to return:* There's one table per day or weekend. Cards rotate and the knife's speed changes (slow Sundays). The sibling ledger gains a "first to stop" column. The knife keeps moving whether you look or not.

*Borrowed logic:* The moving-knife procedure of fair division, the continuous cousin of "I cut, you choose": a knife sweeps the cake, and whoever calls stop first takes the piece behind it.
