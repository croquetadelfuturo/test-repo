# Round 1: C. Systems designer

---

## C1: Point of Balance

**1. One sentence.** You herd five sheep through a trial course without ever touching them, using four whistle commands to a dog whose position pushes on the flock.

**2. 30 seconds of play.**
- **0s:** A top-down hillside, with sheep grazing 300 m away and your dog at your feet. Swipe left sends it circling clockwise, swipe right anticlockwise, swipe up walks it in, and a tap drops it.
- **1s:** You swipe left, and the dog runs a wide arc up the field.
- **7s:** It reaches the far side of the flock and you tap. It drops, and the sheep bunch together.
- **9s:** You swipe up. The dog walks in and the flock moves away from it, toward you, bending left around a gorse bush.
- **14s:** A short swipe right and a tap shift the dog sideways. The flock swings back onto line and through the first gates.
- **25s:** A young ewe drifts toward clover. If you send the dog to cover her, the whole flock will turn.

**3. Central decision and how the game responds.** You choose where to hold the dog and how close. Each sheep follows three rules: move away from a dog inside her flight zone, stay near the flock, drift toward grass. Together they make the flock move away from the dog (the "point of balance"). A closer dog moves them faster, but if it gets too close to a nervous sheep she bolts and the flock splits. A dog that hangs back lets them graze off line. You read all this only from the flock's shape.

**4. Interaction or surprise.** At the pen, your left thumb must hold the gate rope (a press on the screen edge). Lift it and the gate swings shut, so you give every command one-handed. One sheep is a hidden lead ewe. With the dog at 8 m, the flock walks. At 4 m, the lead ewe trots, and the other four follow her instead of fleeing the dog. If she is facing a fence corner, all five pack into it. Drawing them out takes a 270° flank, about 15 of your 180 seconds, and the judge deducts for every metre off line.

**5. What changes between rounds, and the reason to return.** Ground changes (steep hill, flat paddock, a stream the sheep won't cross unless pushed), and so do breeds: hill sheep bolt at 15 m, while heavy ewes let the dog within 4 m and then turn to stamp at it. In a gale, whistles arrive 0.5 s late. Your young dog picks up your habits: keep over-flanking and it starts running wide on its own. A shared daily course shows other players' flock trails.

**6. Borrowed logic.** Sheepdog trials: the outrun–lift–fetch–drive–shed–pen sequence, points deducted from a fixed allowance, whistle-only commands from a fixed post, and holding the pen-gate rope.

## C2: Long Fire

**1. One sentence.** You pack unglazed pots into a sloping tunnel kiln and stoke it through a long firing. The flame and ash follow paths your arrangement creates, and you see the results only when you unbrick the kiln in your next session.

**2. 30 seconds of play.**
- **0s:** A side view of a long kiln climbing a hill, with the firebox at the bottom and the chimney at the top. A tray holds 12 pots.
- **2s:** You drag a tall vase near the firebox. Faint flow lines bend around it, leaving a pale "shadow" behind.
- **5s:** You drop a tea bowl into that shadow and lean a plate against the vase with a two-finger twist.
- **15s:** You swipe across the door to brick it up. The next two minutes stand for five days, and your only readouts are a temperature dial and the chimney smoke.
- **18s:** You tap the stoke hole and a log drops in. Flame flares, the temperature dips then climbs, and black smoke rolls out.
- **22s:** You tap again as the flame pulls back. Tap too soon and the firebox chokes.
- **28s:** A side port halfway up lights. You can now feed the back, but only with logs the front won't get.

**3. Central decision and how the game responds.** You choose where each pot sits and how hard to stoke. Flame takes the easiest path to the chimney. Ash rides the flame and settles on surfaces facing it, most heavily near the firebox, and melts to glaze above 1,280°C. Pots in another pot's shadow get orange blush instead of glaze, and heavy smoke darkens iron-rich clay. Pack the front too tightly and the back starves, leaving chalky, underfired pots.

**4. Interaction or surprise.** Three vases set across the front row with 1 cm gaps act as three jets. The flame speeds through each gap and paints a vertical green stripe of glaze on the bowl behind. You were expecting a plain brown bowl and get three stripes. Ash also pools in the plate leaning on the middle vase, melting into green glass 3 mm deep. The plate is tilted 12°, past the game's 10° limit, so the glaze runs off the rim and fuses the plate to the shelf. You lose the plate, but the shelf keeps the drip, and next firing whatever stands there gets a green foot.

**5. What changes between rounds, and the reason to return.** The kiln keeps its history: walls glaze over and hold more heat, and shelves collect drips and warp. Pine gives fast heat and heavy ash, while oak gives long coals. Iron-rich clay blushes red, and porcelain turns pale green under ash. Unloading waits for your next session, once the kiln has cooled. Buyers ask for things like "a tea bowl with one drip on the left", which you can only aim for through arrangement.

**6. Borrowed logic.** Anagama wood-firing: loading for flame path and ash fall, a multi-day stoking rhythm that sets heat and smoke, and the slow cool before unloading.

## C3: Water Temple

**1. One sentence.** As a valley's water-temple priest, you set the planting calendars of only two rice terraces each year, the other terraces copy whichever neighbour did best, and water and pests travel through the valley as a result.

**2. 30 seconds of play.**
- **0s:** A river runs down the screen past nine terrace blocks. Each block has a 12-month ring, with green arcs for planted months.
- **2s:** You drag a decree seal onto block 5 and slide its arc from months 3–6 to months 5–8. A blue bar beside the river, showing the water left after each block, turns amber below block 5.
- **8s:** You use your second seal on the weir below block 6, swiping it half-closed.
- **12s:** You tap "Run year" and twelve months play out in ten seconds. Fields flood, turn green, then gold. Brown specks (pests) appear in planted fields and hop into planted neighbours.
- **22s:** Block 4 sits flooded and unplanted for two months, and its specks die. Harvest numbers rise, and arrows show who will copy whom next year.

**3. Central decision and how the game responds.** You choose which two blocks to steer, and whether toward planting in sync or staggering. Planted fields draw water, most heavily in their first two months, and blocks downstream get what's left. Pests double monthly in a planted block and spread 10% into planted neighbours. Two months unplanted in a row wipe a block's pests out. Each year, a block copies a neighbour's calendar if that neighbour had a better harvest. Planting in sync starves the pests but makes every block thirsty at once. Staggering shares the water but relays pests down the valley.

**4. Interaction or surprise.** In year 4, you decree that block 5 leaves its fields unplanted in months 6–7, the same months as block 6. Block 5's harvest jumps from 41 to 68. The next year, block 4 copies block 5, and the year after, block 3 copies block 4. The sync climbs the valley by itself, one block a year. In dry year 7 it reaches block 2, so five blocks flood in the same two months. Block 6, the lowest of them, gets 50% of its water and harvests 30. It then copies the staggered calendar of block 7 below it, and block 5 copies block 6 a year later. The wave you started upstream now unravels from the bottom.

**5. What changes between rounds, and the reason to return.** Each year is a short round, and a valley runs about 30 years. Valleys differ (forked rivers, a lake that stores rain, a mill that diverts water), and years bring monsoon, drought, or rats that cross two blocks at a time. Some farmers copy only every other year. A shared-river mode puts real players on adjacent blocks.

**6. Borrowed logic.** Balinese subak irrigation and water-temple scheduling: synchronised fallowing for pest control set against staggered planting for water sharing, with farm groups adopting schedules that worked for their neighbours.

## C4: Caller (strange one)

**1. One sentence.** You are the caller at a folk dance. You never move a dancer, only announce figures in time with the band, and the way your calls make dancers mix along the line is the puzzle.

**2. 30 seconds of play.**
- **0s:** Eight couples in coloured hats stand in two facing lines. Below them is a 64-beat timeline with four 16-beat slots, and a hand of figure cards showing diagrams and beat lengths: Swing neighbour (16), Do-si-do partner (8), Robins chain (8), Partner swing (16), Circle left ¾ (8), Pass through (8).
- **2s:** In the walkthrough, before any music, you drag cards into slots and the dancers walk each figure slowly.
- **8s:** You end the sequence with Circle left ¾ and Pass through. Each couple now faces a new group, and trails show odd-numbered couples one place down the line, even-numbered couples one place up.
- **12s:** You tap the fiddler and the reel starts, at 32 seconds per time through. You can still swap any slot that hasn't started, if the card lands two beats early.
- **28s:** The couples at each end step out to wait. A counter reads "new neighbours met: 8".

**3. Central decision and how the game responds.** You choose the next figure, given where the last one left everyone. Each figure is a fixed rearrangement of four dancers. A figure that needs partners side by side, called while they are across the set, makes them collide and lose beats. Every 64 beats your sequence repeats, and its combined effect decides which way each couple travels. Couples reaching an end sit out once and re-enter going the other way. You never move anyone along the line yourself.

**4. Interaction or surprise.** A dancer asks for variety, so you swap Do-si-do partner for Robins chain: same length, prettier. Under this game's rules, a chain sends every robin (one of the two dance roles) across, and a partner swing ends on the robin's side. Every couple now ends each time through on the opposite line, and since a couple's line sets its direction, direction flips every time. Red moves down to Green, back up to its old neighbour, then down to Green again. Nothing collides, and the dance looks lovely. But the counter freezes at 2, and the request "Orange (top) wants to dance with Grey (bottom)" can never be met. The fix is a second chain, and its 8 beats must come out of another figure.

**5. What changes between rounds, and the reason to return.** An evening is 8–10 dances, each a round. Beginners get lost easily, and experienced dancers get bored by repeated figures. Formations change (long lines, four-couple squares), and so do tunes, including "crooked" ones with irregular phrases. Requests arrive ("Purple is tired"), and new figures unlock, such as the hey for four. You can name your own dance and share it for others to call.

**6. Borrowed logic.** Contra dance calling: a 64-beat sequence that repeats with a built-in progression, groups of four, calling each figure before its phrase starts, and couples waiting out at the ends before rejoining in the other direction.
