# Round 2: C (Systems designer) expands D1–D4

---

## D1: Proof

**1. More expressive interaction: the score.** Before baking, you draw the score on the dough with one finger. A slow stroke cuts deep, and a low angle lifts an "ear". Proof decides whether your drawing survives. A well-timed loaf opens along your lines, an underproofed one bursts where the cut was shallowest, and an overproofed one blurs. Customers learn your mark ("the wheat-stalk one"), but they only see it on loaves you timed well.

**2. Unexpected consequence: the focaccia bakery.** Possible rule: feeding the starter irregularly, without ever neglecting it, gives it the trait "sour and fast". A player with an unpredictable day finds that overproof is the only state they can reliably hit, because focaccia has no window. So they start mixing doughs they mean to forget. Focaccia standing orders pile up and make their day even less predictable, and the starter drifts toward "sour and fast", which suits focaccia better still. The town calls them "the focaccia place". The bakery's identity comes from the hours its owner is away.

**3. Branch: D1-b, Friendship Bag**

- **One sentence.** Another player hands you a bag of starter. You tend it on a fixed 10-day schedule, then split it four ways, keeping one portion to bake and giving three away, and every bag carries a family tree of everyone who has fed its line.
- **30 seconds of play.** It's 08:05, and a zip bag labelled "Day 6 · from Tomás · gen 5" sits on the counter. You squeeze it with your thumb (the daily "mush"), and bubbles slide under the plastic. It's feeding day, so you drag in a cup each of flour, sugar and milk, then one pinch from your spice rack: cardamom. You tap the bag's label, and a family tree unfolds back to an origin bag from 41 days ago. It has 212 live bags, plus dead branches in grey.
- **Central decision and response.** You choose what to add, and who gets your three portions: trusted friends, or the "shelf", where strangers pick bags up. A bag left unmushed for 2 days dies. A pinch added in two consecutive generations becomes a fixed trait of the line. Lemon and milk together curdle 1 time in 4. The game answers only through the tree, as branches live, die or merge.
- **Surprise.** Five weeks later, "Day 1 · from Kai · gen 9" arrives. It's your own line coming back: a shelf bag went through a stranger and four more kitchens to reach a friend of yours. It carries your cardamom, now fixed because the next keeper added cardamom too, plus rye and orange you never chose. The loaf tastes like nothing any single player designed.
- **Between rounds and reason to return.** Each 10-day cycle is a round, and you can tend several bags at once. New origin lines appear each season. The bag needs one squeeze a day, and your branches keep living or dying in other people's kitchens.
- **Borrowed logic.** Amish Friendship Bread: a bagged starter passed hand to hand on a 10-day schedule (mush daily, feed on day 6, divide on day 10, keep one portion, give three away).

## D2: Radio Sked

**1. More expressive interaction: written orders.** At a call you can build a standing order from chips, such as IF [wind < 30] AND [O₂ > 50%] THEN [push to C4] ELSE [hold]. Each chip costs 3 seconds of airtime from a radio battery that holds 120 seconds and loses 20 on each night below −20°C. A careful order today means a shorter call on summit day. Some players send one-chip orders that trust Dawa, while others send contracts that Mira still reads generously.

**2. Unexpected consequence: the empty window.** Crowds on the ridge add queue time, so the forecast's best window stops being best once everyone reads it. Players learn to pick the second-best window until it becomes the crowded one, and over the seasons the popular window swings back and forth. A player who always picks the "bad" window becomes known for empty-ridge photos, and others check where that expedition's tents are before choosing.

**3. Branch: D2-b, Grey Line**

- **One sentence.** You run a radio station on a remote island and pass written messages around the world. Each path opens only at certain hours as day and night move across the globe, so every message either waits for its window or hops through other players' stations.
- **30 seconds of play.** At 06:02, a world map shows the night side shaded, with a bright band where night meets day (the "grey line"). Your station is on Kerguelen. The top slip in your tray reads "To Valparaíso: BABY BORN BOTH WELL (4 words)". You turn the band dial to 20 m, and arcs light toward stations you can reach now: Perth bright, Cape Town faint, Valparaíso dark. Perth is another player, booked to talk to Valparaíso at 21:45. You drag the slip onto Perth, then pin your next sked for 05:50 tomorrow, when the grey line returns.
- **Central decision and response.** You choose whether to send a message now through relays, or hold it for a direct path later. Each band reaches different regions at different hours, and paths along the grey line are strongest. Every hop needs a "sked", a time booked by both players. Each hop may garble one word unless the receiver taps "check", which costs them 30 seconds of their window. Delivered messages show their route on the map and their changed words in red.
- **Surprise.** Your slip for Mexico City is routed through three hops and will take 20 hours. But at 06:10, your dawn and Mexico City's dusk fall on the same grey line, and 20 m opens the long way round the planet for 35 minutes. You deliver it direct while the relay copy still sits in Perth. Elsewhere, a slip that went through four unchecked hops arrives as "BABY BORN BOAT WELL".
- **Between rounds and reason to return.** The grey line tilts with the seasons, so openings shift through the year, and storms can close polar paths for a day. Players are waiting at skeds you booked, with slips that depend on your window.
- **Borrowed logic.** Amateur HF radio: grey-line and long-path propagation, bands that open at certain times of day, booked skeds, and traffic nets that relay written messages with a word-count check.

## D3: Statement

**1. More expressive interaction: the free sketch.** Before the first question, you may place up to five details on the pencil outline. You drag a figure to where you saw them, tint it, and draw an arrow for how they moved. Correct details are inked and become "sworn", which means leading questions can't plant anything about them. Wrong ones stay in pencil, and the investigator builds later questions on them. Your sketch shows which memories you trusted first.

**2. Unexpected consequence: the town's green umbrella.** Assume the 21:00 line-up is visible to players who haven't answered yet. Waiting for the 3× multiplier then means answering after you've seen the line-up. On Thursday it shows 58% remembering the planted umbrella as green. Late answerers who are unsure follow the crowd, and by midnight the figure is 71%. Players defend the green umbrella in chat by quoting each other. Then Friday's replay shows empty pavement.

**3. Branch: D3-b, Open Question**

- **One sentence.** You are the investigator for an incident at the corner, which you never saw. Five witnesses each saw part of it, and their memories fade and absorb whatever is said to them, so you choose who to question, in what order and in what words.
- **30 seconds of play.** A map shows five witness portraits where they stood, each with a pale view cone and a memory bar that slowly drains. A clock reads "2 h since incident". You tap the newsagent. The question builder offers three kinds of question (open, probing, leading) and a context card ("Picture the bakery smell") that helps him recall but costs 2 of his 10 patience. You play the context card, then ask an open question. Details drop onto the map with confidence rings: grey cap 0.7, paper bag 0.4. A dotted line shows him sharing the waiting bench with the cyclist for the next 15 minutes.
- **Central decision and response.** You choose the order and wording of your questions, and who waits together. Memories decay over in-game hours. Open questions retrieve details without changing them. A leading question plants its detail at strength 0.3, and each repeat adds 0.2. Witnesses who share the bench swap their two strongest details. Separate rooms prevent that, but cost 20 minutes each.
- **Surprise.** You ask the cyclist one leading question: "Did he run toward the tram?" She says "maybe". On the bench, she passes it to the newsagent, who saw nothing on that side, so it fills a blank in his memory. An hour later he says firmly, "He ran for the tram." Two witnesses agree, and it's the strongest detail in your file. At the reveal, the footage plays under your reconstruction, and one coloured thread runs from your question through her to him.
- **Between rounds and reason to return.** There's a new case each day, with different witnesses: a suggestible teenager, a retired officer who embellishes, a tourist whose translator paraphrases. Your record counts how many details in your files started in your own mouth.
- **Borrowed logic.** The police cognitive interview: free recall before specific questions, recreating the context of the event, avoiding leading questions, and keeping witnesses apart so they don't contaminate each other.

## D4: Held Note (strange one)

**1. More expressive interaction: the glide.** Tapping the new pitch changes the note cleanly. Sliding your thumb from the old pitch to the new one makes a glide that lasts 10 real minutes per second of slide. A 3-second slide from E to G takes half an hour and passes through F♯ on the way. A glide can slip past a clash in an older layer, or move through one on purpose as tension that resolves.

**2. Unexpected consequence: the 3 a.m. duet.** In a duet, your friend's layers play under yours, so you hear their week. A thin, high note from 02:10 to 02:50 on three nights means they aren't sleeping. The loop repeats every 24 hours, so you can answer it. Tonight you choose a bedtime note that harmonises with theirs. It will still be sounding at 02:10, keeping them company in playback while you're both asleep.

**3. Branch: D4-b, Peal**

- **One sentence.** Six players each own one bell in a tower. Every time you check your phone you pull your rope once, deciding whether your bell swaps with a neighbour or keeps its place. A new row sounds when all six have pulled, and the band tries to ring every possible order without repeating one.
- **30 seconds of play.** *Assume D4's lock-screen widget.* At 10:47 the widget shows the current row, 2 1 4 3 6 5, with your bell, the 4, in gold. Behind each bell runs its "line", a zigzag through earlier rows. Ada (bell 1), next to you, has already pointed her arrow at you. You tap the matching arrow, and the 1 and the 4 cross with a chime. Two ringers haven't pulled yet. At 11:20, the last one checks his phone, and all six phones play 2 4 1 6 3 5.
- **Central decision and response.** You choose whether to swap or keep your place, and whether to follow the method. A bell moves at most one position per row. A swap happens only if both bells choose it, and if their choices don't match, both stay. Any repeated row makes the "touch" (the ringing so far) false. The method gives each ringer a local rule. In Plain Hunt you keep moving one way and pause for a row at each end, and the conductor's "calls" can change the rule briefly. Each ringer gets one pull per row, so the slowest phone-checker sets the pace.
- **Surprise.** Following only Plain Hunt, six strangers ring 12 different rows and come back to rounds (1 2 3 4 5 6) without exchanging a word. Plain Bob extends the course to 60 rows, and with calls it reaches all 720. On day 9, bell 2 keeps its place when it should have swapped, and nothing sounds wrong. Days later the band repeats an old row, the tower glows red, and the lines show the kink where it started.
- **Between rounds and reason to return.** Towers have 4, 5 or 6 bells (24, 120 or 720 possible rows), and new methods unlock. The row can't sound until you pull, and your bell's line is a drawing only you can continue.
- **Borrowed logic.** English change ringing: one ringer per bell, swaps only between adjacent positions, Plain Hunt and Plain Bob with calls from a conductor, and a "true" touch that never repeats a row.
