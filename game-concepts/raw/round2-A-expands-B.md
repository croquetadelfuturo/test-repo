# Round 2: A (Interaction inventor) expands B (Information designer)

## B1. The Skerry Forecast

### 1. More expressive interaction: the forecast brush
Instead of using the dials, you can paint each question's forecast onto an hour strip running 08:00–18:00. **Finger pressure sets the probability:** a light touch lays a 30% wash and a firm press builds to 90%. **A thumb smudge softens an edge in time.** A hard edge at 15:00 says "rain at three". A long fade says "sometime this afternoon".

Each hour is scored separately, and villagers read the shape. A hard edge brings the fisherman in at 14:30, while a fade keeps him in harbour all day. Two equally calibrated forecasters end up with noticeboards you can tell apart, one blocky like a woodcut and one in watercolour.

### 2. Unexpected consequence: bold calls blind you
*Possible rule:* boats at sea radio wind and swell every hour, so each boat is a moving station.

Villagers only act at 80% or more, so an honest gale call ties up every boat, and on the stormiest days your western observations go dark. Players find a grim trick: post 75%, so that one skipper sails and keeps reporting. If a gale catches him, his family ignores your noticeboard for a week. Honest numbers and a full observation network pull against each other, and every stormy morning you decide whether old Hamish is worth the risk.

### 3. B1-b. Glass
**The phone's pressure sensor becomes a household barometer: each morning you tap the glass, look at your real sky and give one honest percentage for rain where you live, and the real day scores it.**

*Assume the phone has a pressure sensor and the game can learn afterwards whether rain fell nearby.*

**30 seconds of play**
- **0–8 s:** Tap the brass dial's glass twice. The needle twitches down a hair, the nudge real owners give to free a sticky needle. Yesterday's set hand reads 1018 and the needle reads 1012.
- **8–18 s:** Hold the phone up to the actual sky and pick the matching cloud icon: "sun through ground glass".
- **18–30 s:** Drag the set hand over the needle, set "Rain here before midnight?" to 70% and seal it. A barograph drum starts inking the day.

**Central decision**
You give one number from three sources: the pressure trend, your sky, and the rhymes engraved on the dial ("Long foretold, long last; short notice, soon past"). At midnight the real outcome becomes a dot on your reliability chart, and each rhyme you trusted gains a tally.

**Surprise**
The barometer is also an altimeter. A lift to the 10th floor (about 30 m) pulls the needle down 3.6 hPa, the drum draws a cliff, and new players announce storms that were lifts. Commutes ink spikes at 08:10 and 18:20, and the skill becomes reading the slow real fall underneath your own movements.

**Between rounds / reason to return**
- Your town's real seasons change the weather you forecast.
- Each drum sheet covers a week, and the sheets bind into a yearly book.
- Friends trade sheets. A friend 200 km west whose line falls on Tuesday is showing you Wednesday's front.

**Borrowed logic:** The home barometer: tapping the glass, the set hand, pressure tendency, weather rhymes and the weekly barograph drum.

## B2. Stemma

### 1. More expressive interaction: conjecture and the dagger
You can write a word that no copy has.
- **Conjecture:** trace it letter by letter on the Reconstruction line and attach the error that explains every copy's failure. For example, a minim confusion turned "hinde" into "hmde". The edition prints it as *hinde* conj. [your name].
- **Daggers:** if a passage defeats you, wrap it in daggers: †hmde at†. Daggers are never wrong and cost little.

Some editors dagger cautiously and some emend boldly. A later fragment that confirms your conjecture gets you credited in the chronicle. One that disproves it leaves your struck-through word in the edition's notes for good.

### 2. Unexpected consequence: the forger who read the manual
"Prefer the harder reading" can be exploited. In Scribe Mode, a player who knows the method can plant archaic words ("herne", "quhen", "eke") so solvers promote their copy to the top of the tree.

*Possible rule for spotting it:* genuine old copies carry hard readings alongside ordinary wear, such as dropped letters and doubled words. A forgery's hard readings sit in suspiciously clean text.

Forgery-spotting lore grows, the abbey chronicle's mystery could be a 17th-century forger who did exactly this, and players learn to recognise each other's natural slips like handwriting.

### 3. B2-b. Palimpsest
**A page of parchment was scraped and reused; you tilt the phone to rake light across it and trace the erased text one stroke at a time.**

**30 seconds of play**
- **0–8 s:** A psalter page. Tilt the phone and a lamp swings low across it. At about 8°, faint pen grooves appear running **sideways**, because the original book was turned 90° when it was reused.
- **8–15 s:** Rotate the page with two fingers until the grooves run level.
- **15–25 s:** Switch on the UV lamp and the scraped ink glows violet. Trace a stroke with your fingertip and it locks in as "h", then "e", then three identical upright strokes.
- **25–30 s:** "rn", "m" or "in"? You pick "rn", and the lamp's budget ticks down.

**Central decision**
You decide which strokes belong to the lower text and how to read the ambiguous ones. The upper ink always sits on top where the layers cross. Each committed letter suggests its neighbours from a word list, and a wrong choice shows up as nonsense three letters later.

**Surprise**
Each page has a **light budget** of 50 lux-hours, the conservator's limit, and a spent page goes into storage for a week. Once you have traced five confident examples of the scribe's "g", one tap highlights every faint "g" on the page. That can open a whole line, or mislead you if one of the five was really a "q".

**Between rounds / reason to return**
The lower texts are scattered across many reused books, bound out of order. A line cut off on one page continues on a page found weeks later, and you rebuild the lost book from those joins. Some pages hold three layers.

**Borrowed logic:** Palimpsest recovery: raking light shows pen indentations, UV reveals scraped ink, light exposure is rationed, and you learn the scribe's letterforms.

## B3. Segregable

### 1. More expressive interaction: markers and brackets
You choose a tool for each cut.
- **Fat marker:** fast, but it overshoots half a character at each end.
- **Fine marker:** precise, but a single quick swipe streaks and lets the tops of tall letters show.
- **Bracket:** replace a span with a descriptor built from tiles, such as "[a resident]" or "[a woman]". A bracket costs half the Release loss of a bar, but its words are evidence. "[a woman]" quietly removes Teodor from the list.

Cover "Ilse at the" with one fine stroke and the tops of "I" and "l" still peek through at positions 1–2, so the pen pins Ilse anyway. Some reviewers leave readable prose full of brackets; others leave solid slabs.

### 2. Unexpected consequence: releasing more can protect
Your own releases feed the archive, so your past self becomes the requester's best source. The mosaic also runs backwards: a fragment only narrows things down if it is rare.

*Possible rule:* a chain goes cold when it leaves three or more candidates.

Releasing the full harbour log, with nine passengers off the last Thursday boat, turns "arrived on the last boat" from a fingerprint into a crowd. Sometimes the best protection is a document you could have withheld. The town's archive grows oddly full in some years and silent in others, and sharp readers find the secrets by where the generosity clusters.

### 3. B3-b. Passed by Censor
**You write two-sided letters home from a wartime port, arranging phrase tiles so your family can decode where you are, while a censor's razor cuts out anything forbidden, and every cut also removes whatever was on the back.**

**30 seconds of play**
- **0–8 s:** A tile bank holds "Give my love to Aunt May", "weather foul" and "xx". Your family code card says *Aunt May = coast; kisses = weeks until leave*. The censor's list says *No places, ships, weather, dates*.
- **8–20 s:** Drag the Aunt May line to line 3 and add "xxx". Swipe the sheet over and place "Tell Dad the dog sounds well" on the back. Fold and seal.
- **20–30 s:** The censor's razor cuts out "weather foul", and the rectangle falls out with a snick. Turn the sheet over: the same hole took half your kisses.

**Central decision**
You decide where each clue sits on the two sides, and how obvious the code is.

*Possible rule:* a phrase that passes three times goes on the censor's watch list.

Your family's reply shows what they understood: "So glad you're seeing Aunt May!"

**Surprise**
Bait on purpose. Put a forbidden line directly behind a back-side phrase you want gone, perhaps a lie meant for a nosy reader at home, and the censor's cut deletes it for you.

**Between rounds / reason to return**
Each week brings a new posting and new correspondents with their own codes, such as a sister who counts capital letters. Their replies come back censored too, and the correspondence becomes the story.

**Borrowed logic:** Wartime postal censorship: razor cuts that remove both sides of the sheet, lists of forbidden topics, and families' pre-arranged codes.

## B4. Assumes Facts

### 1. More expressive interaction: the exhibit sketch
"Show the court where everything was." Drag tokens for the van, the dog, the bus stop and the man in green onto a blank street plan.
- **Short press = pencil:** approximate, can't be used against you, sways the jury less.
- **Long press = ink:** firm testimony that can be impeached.
- **Drag a finger:** draws a route, such as the man's path out of the shop.

Some witnesses ink three things and pencil the rest; some ink everything. Cross-examination reads your ink: "You placed the van two metres from the bin, yet agreed it smashed into it."

### 2. Unexpected consequence: base-rate witnesses
Scenes are built from a library of parts, so parts have frequencies. After 50 trials, players notice that corner shops have a dog 60% of the time and vans are usually white. Some stop remembering and start answering from the statistics.

Lawyers adapt. *Possible rule:* a "schema" lawyer plants only parts that appear in at least half of scenes of that type, such as "Did the cashier hand him his change?" in a shop that had no cashier. The game ends up separating remembering from guessing what is usually there, and at appeal your opponent is often your own expectations.

### 3. B4-b. Likeness
**You are the police composite artist: you question a witness and build a face feature by feature, but every leading question and every look at your draft bends her memory toward your drawing.**

*Assume a face generator that blends features into believable faces.*

**30 seconds of play**
- **0–6 s:** Dot, 71, saw a bag snatch two days ago. Her face sits in a small window, with a blank face and feature trays on your desk.
- **6–14 s:** Play the open card "Picture the bus shelter. What was the weather?" She says: "Drizzle. His hood was up… no, a cap."
- **14–22 s:** Drag on a cap. "Broad face," she says, so you pinch the jaw wider.
- **22–30 s:** You give in to "Did he have a moustache?" She says "…maybe?", and a faint moustache flickers on her confidence portrait.

**Central decision**
You choose the kind of question and when to show the draft. Open questions give fewer details but clean ones. Closed questions are faster, but each one pulls her hidden memory toward your suggestion. Showing the draft earns corrections ("eyes further apart"), but each viewing pulls every feature of her memory about 20% toward your drawing.

**Surprise**
Next day there is a line-up of six. Show the draft three or more times and Dot confidently picks number 4, who resembles your composite, not the real thief, number 2. The reveal morphs from the true face to your composite to her memory after each viewing.

**Between rounds / reason to return**
- New witnesses: a suggestible child, a bus driver who remembers vehicles but not faces, a certain, mistaken retiree.
- Later cases make you choose the line-up's fillers. If none of them wears a cap, the suspect stands out.
- Case files build into a unit record.

**Borrowed logic:** Police composite construction (feature kits), the cognitive interview (open prompts and reinstating the context), and line-up rules that require fillers to match the description.
